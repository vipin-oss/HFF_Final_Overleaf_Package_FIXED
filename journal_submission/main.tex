\begin{structuredabstract}
\abshead{Purpose}
This paper derives and verifies a closed model of a flux-heated channel wall that deforms
under its own temperature field, and quantifies the feedback of that deformation on the
conjugate flow and heat transfer it bounds.

\abshead{Design/methodology/approach}
Plane-strain thermoelasticity is reduced to an axially restrained beam--column strip by a
two-field variational principle, the flow is reduced in the lubrication limit, and the
thermal-entry problem is solved by a Sturm--Liouville expansion. Closed forms are obtained for
the hydraulic resistance (a Gauss hypergeometric function), the bow and the buckling
threshold; the axial-compatibility nonlinearity is proved to be a contraction. A
finite-element solution of the coupled problem verifies each formula and measures its error.

\abshead{Findings}
At $\ReDh=50$ and $q''=5\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$ the strip bows $0.441\,H_0$ and the pressure drop
rises from $144$ to $315$~Pa. The closed form $\Delta p/\Delta p_0={}_2F_1(3,\tfrac12;1;a)$
reproduces this to $0.03\%$, whereas the $\sin^2$ idealisation underpredicts it by $15\%$. The
wall rise is $34.3$~K, $6.2\%$ above one-dimensional conduction. The frozen-force closure
overpredicts the bow by $1.2\%$ at the design point and $14.9\%$ at $10\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$.
Deformation raises the local Nusselt number by up to $70\%$, with exponent $0.79$ in the
stretch $H_0/H$.

\abshead{Research limitations/implications}
Plane strain, steady laminar constant-property flow, small slopes, $|w|/H_0\le0.45$;
transverse shear is omitted at an estimated $4\%$; no experiment is reported.

\abshead{Practical implications}
Design formulae replace semi-empirical corrections; deformation, not strength or buckling,
sets the operating range.

\abshead{Originality/value}
The loop is closed in closed form and every formula is verified against an independent
computation.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} conjugate heat transfer; thermoelastic stability;
fluid--structure interaction; lubrication theory; Sturm--Liouville series;
verification

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Channels whose walls are thin enough to bend are the rule rather than the
exception in compact heat exchangers and in microfluidic hardware. In a
single-phase microchannel heat sink the wall between the coolant and the heat
source is frequently of the same order as the passage itself: the pumping
penalties of such geometries are documented in the classical design reviews
\citep{garimella2004single} and in measurements of friction and heat transfer
in straight and finned microchannels \citep{dey2012frictional,
gorasiya2018single,ruiz2015experimental,eltaweel2023experimental,
han2026experimental}, and the review of nanofluid and single-phase
microchannel data by \citet{eneren2022experiments} makes the same point from
the experimental side --- the wall is not a passive boundary, and its
thickness and conductivity enter the measured Nusselt number through axial
conduction. Once the wall is also compliant, as it is in polymer devices,
additively manufactured cold plates and thin-walled instrumented test
sections, the passage height becomes an unknown of the problem rather than a
datum.

The mechanical consequence of a compliant wall has been analysed
extensively for pressure-driven deformation. \citet{gervais2006flow}
measured flow-induced deformation of shallow microchannels and derived a
coupled flow-rate relation; \citet{christov2018flow} obtained the
flow rate--pressure drop relation for a deformable shallow channel in a form
that is now used as a benchmark; \citet{wang2019theory} extended the theory to
thick walls of finite compliance, and \citet{boyko2022flow} recast the same
class of problems through fluidic and elastic reciprocal theorems. In a
turbulent channel \citet{zhang2017deformation} quantified the change of the
mean profile produced by a compliant wall. In all of these studies the load
that deforms the wall is the pressure, and the energy equation is either
absent or passive.

A second and, for heated hardware, more consequential coupling arises when
the deformation is produced by the temperature field that the wall itself
conducts. The literature on fluid--structure interaction with heat transfer is
large, and it is dominated by cavity configurations in which a flexible wall
or fin interacts with a buoyant or shear-driven flow:
\citet{soti2015flow} showed that the deformation of a thin flexible
structure can enhance heat transfer; \citet{alamiri2011fluid} analysed mixed
convection in a lid-driven cavity with a flexible bottom wall;
\citet{alsabery2019transient,alsabery2019entropy} treated transient
convection and entropy generation with a flexible right wall;
\citet{zargartalebi2018fluid} studied buoyancy-driven flow past a flexible
partition; \citet{saleh2025impact} examined unsteady flow in a wavy-heated
cavity; \citet{gong2025effects} and \citet{ahmed2024enhancing} considered
flexible walls and fins in natural convection; \citet{chowdhury2025fluid}
studied a thin flexible heater in a non-Newtonian fluid;
\citet{derakhshandeh2026fluid} quantified the effect of flexible fins on
laminar channel heat transfer; \citet{benhamida2022fluid} treated a T-shaped
cavity with a flexible baffle; \citet{kumar2026nonlinear} reported nonlinear
interactions of two cantilever plates in a differentially heated cavity; and
\citet{turek2006proposal} provided the benchmark that most of these codes are
compared against. The common structure of these studies is that the fluid
supplies the load and the solid responds; the wall temperature is a
by-product.

The complementary configuration --- a wall that is heated through its
thickness and deforms because of the resulting eigenstrain, with the flow
responding to the changed passage --- is the classical subject of
thermoelasticity rather than of fluid--structure interaction. The bending and
curling of a heated thin plate was treated by \citet{mansfield1962bending};
the buckling of cylindrical shells heated along an axial strip by
\citet{hoff1964buckling} and \citet{ross1965buckling}; more recent work
addresses thermal post-buckling of annular sector plates
\citep{mehrabi2025axisymmetric} and generalised magneto-thermoelasticity of
layers and cylinders \citep{sadeghi2022generalized,karimipour2023lord,
gupta2024photo}. The monographs of \citet{boley1960theory},
\citet{timoshenko1970elasticity} and \citet{timoshenko1961stability} supply
the eigenstrain and stability relations used in the present reduction.

The thermal side of the same problem has its own literature. Conjugate heat
transfer with a conducting wall of finite thickness has been studied for
thick-walled flattened channels \citep{rahimi2024numerical}, for turbulent
wall jets with an imposed flux \citep{vishnuvardhanarao2009computational},
for porous enclosures with thick walls \citep{varol2009conjugate}, for
phase-change storage units \citep{li1996conjugate}, for eccentric annuli
\citep{elshaarawi2001critical} and for parallel-plate channels with a step
change in the thermal boundary condition \citep{hassab2013effect}. Exact
solutions that retain both axial and transverse wall conduction are available
for counterflow parallel-plate exchangers \citep{quintero2017laminar}, and the
effect of wall axial conduction on the measured effectiveness of concentric
tube exchangers is quantified by \citet{arici2010heat}. These studies
establish two facts that matter here: the wall conduction problem is
essentially one-dimensional through the thickness when $k_s d \ll k_f L$, and
axial conduction in the wall, while small in the core of a heated segment,
produces end effects over a length $O(d)$ that a reduced model cannot
represent.

The three strands above have not, to the best of the present author's
knowledge, been closed into a single verified computation for the
configuration that is most common in practice: a narrow heated passage whose
upper wall is a thin, axially restrained strip, heated on its outer face, and
free to bow into the passage. In that configuration the loop is genuinely
closed, because the wall temperature that drives the deformation is itself
set by the convective resistance of the passage whose height the deformation
controls. The loop has three properties that make it worth isolating.

First, the driving mechanism is thermal, not hydraulic. The pressure-only bow
of the present strip is $9.2\times10^{-8}$~m against a thermal bow of
$2.2\times10^{-4}$~m, a ratio of $1.7\times10^{3}$, so the deformation is
thermoelastic for practical purposes while the hydraulic consequence of the
deformation is first order ($\Delta p$ more than doubles). A pressure-driven
analysis would therefore get the mechanism wrong and the consequence right by
accident, which is the least useful combination.

Second, the amplitude is controlled by geometric rather than by material
nonlinearity. The strip is axially restrained, so bowing relieves the
compressive membrane force through the arc-length condition; the resulting
amplification of the linear bow is $40\%$ at the design point and grows
without bound as the blocked load approaches the Euler load. A linear
thermoelastic analysis cannot represent this.

Third, the reduced wall model has an explicit ceiling: the small-slope
kinematics and the body-fitted mesh construction both fail when the bow
approaches the passage height, which for the present geometry happens inside
the range of fluxes that an application would use. The useful engineering
output of the analysis is therefore not a single number but the location of
that ceiling together with the accuracies that can be claimed below it.

This paper makes five contributions.
(i) A complete and self-consistent statement of the coupled problem with the
quantitative support of every assumption, including the dimensionless groups
and the five regime bounds that delimit it (\S\ref{sec:model}--\S\ref{sec:groups}).
(ii) A full derivation of the reduced wall model from three-dimensional
linear thermoelasticity, including the through-thickness temperature profile,
the eigen-curvature, the blocked force, the axial-compatibility condition and
the closed-form solution used for verification (\S\ref{sec:wall} and
Appendix~\ref{app:closedform}).
(iii) A computational formulation in which the fluid and wall energy
equations are assembled into one algebraic system on a conforming interface,
so that temperature continuity and the conjugate flux condition hold exactly
at the discrete level, together with the coupling algorithm and its stopping
rules (\S\ref{sec:numerics}).
(iv) A verification and grid-convergence study with the Grid Convergence
Index of Roache \citep{roache1994perspective} on five quantities of interest,
following the verification-and-validation framework of
\citet{oberkampf2002verification} (\S\ref{sec:grid}, \S\ref{sec:verification}).
(v) The coupled solution at a design point, its sensitivity to the imposed
flux, the wall thickness, the Reynolds number and two material properties,
the location of the kinematic, strength and stability ceilings, and the
model-form error of the frozen-force wall closure (\S\ref{sec:results},
\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\S\ref{sec:limitations} states what the model excludes; the reduced
structural model is the most consequential of those exclusions and it is not
presented as a substitute for a two-dimensional thermoelastic continuum.

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:equations} sets out the field
equations, the dimensionless groups and the limits against which the solution is later
checked, and the nomenclature follows it. Section~\ref{sec:model} poses the problem: the
configuration, the ten assumptions with their quantitative support, the derivation of the
reduced wall model and the boundary data. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} describes how the
coupled problem is solved, including the monolithic conjugate assembly, the coupling
algorithm, the mesh-independence study and the verification of the implementation.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the coupled solution and its sensitivity to five
parameters, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the results mean and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what the model excludes. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}
concludes. Three appendices record the closed-form algebra, the weak forms and the
reproduction record.

\section{Field equations}
\label{sec:equations}

\subsection{Field equations and kinematics}
\label{sec:field}

With $\bu=(u,v)$ the velocity, $p$ the pressure, $T_f$ the fluid temperature,
$T_s$ the solid temperature and $\theta=T_s-T_{\mathrm{in}}$ the wall
temperature rise, the steady field equations are
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\bu &= 0 && \text{in } \Om_f, \label{eq:continuity}\\
\rho\,(\bu\cdot\nabla)\bu &= -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\bu && \text{in } \Om_f, \label{eq:momentum}\\
\rho c_p\,(\bu\cdot\nabla)T_f &= k_f\nabla^2 T_f && \text{in } \Om_f, \label{eq:fluidenergy}\\
\nabla\cdot(k_s\nabla T_s) &= 0 && \text{in } \Om_s^0, \label{eq:solidenergy}
\end{align}
in which $\Om_f$ is the \emph{deformed} fluid domain, whose upper boundary on
$0\le x\le L$ is the deflected interface, while the wall conduction problem
is posed on the reference configuration $\Om_s^0$ (\S\ref{sec:wallmodelscope}).
Buoyancy and viscous dissipation are absent from
\eqref{eq:momentum}--\eqref{eq:fluidenergy} for the quantitative reasons given
in \S\ref{sec:assumptions}. The fluid properties are those of
Table~\ref{tab:params}; the transport equations are the standard
constant-property forms \citep{bird1960transport}, and the wall equation is
the steady conduction equation of \citet{carslaw1947conduction}.

\subsection{Thermoelastic constitutive law and equilibrium}
\label{sec:constitutive}

The wall is described by the Duhamel--Neumann extension of isotropic linear
elasticity,
\begin{align}
\bsig &= 2\mu_L\beps + \lambda(\operatorname{tr}\beps)\bI - \beta\theta\bI,
\qquad \beta=(3\lambda+2\mu_L)\alpha=\frac{E\alpha}{1-2\nu},
\label{eq:constitutive}\\
\beps &= \tfrac12\bigl(\nabla\bd+\nabla\bd^{\mathsf T}\bigr),
\qquad \nabla\cdot\bsig=\mathbf 0 \ \ \text{in } \Om_s^0,
\label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{align}
where $\lambda,\mu_L$ are the Lam\'e parameters, $\bd$ the displacement and
$\beta$ the thermal modulus \citep{boley1960theory,timoshenko1970elasticity}.
Equation \eqref{eq:equilibrium} is posed on the reference configuration,
which is consistent with assumption A4; the fluid equations
\eqref{eq:continuity}--\eqref{eq:fluidenergy} are posed on the deformed domain
$\Om_f$ and therefore carry the geometric coupling of the problem.

\subsection{Variational reduction to the strip}
\label{sec:variational}

The one-dimensional model of \S\ref{sec:wall} is not postulated: it is the Euler--Lagrange
system of a two-field functional obtained from the three-dimensional virtual work of the
strip. Let $u_0(x)$ be the axial displacement of the mid-surface and $w(x)$ the deflection
into the passage. Under the Kirchhoff hypothesis the displacement field and the axial strain
are
\begin{equation}
u_x=u_0(x)-y'w'(x),\qquad u_y=w(x),\qquad
\eps_{xx}=\eps^{0}-y'w'',\qquad
\eps^{0}=u_0'+\tfrac12 w'^2 ,
\label{eq:kirchhoff}
\end{equation}
with $y'$ measured from the mid-surface; the last term of $\eps^{0}$ is the geometric
shortening that couples stretching to bending and that a linear theory omits. The
plane-strain free energy of the strip, with the thermal strain subtracted, is
\begin{equation}
\Pi[u_0,w]=\frac12\int_0^L\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}
\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\bigl[\eps_{xx}-(1+\nu)\alpha\theta\bigr]^2\,\dd y'\,\dd x
+\frac12\int_0^L D_s\bigl(w''+\kappa_{th}\bigr)^2\dd x-\int_0^L f\,w\,\dd x ,
\label{eq:functional}
\end{equation}
where the second integral is the bending energy written in terms of the curvature measured
from its stress-free value $\kappa_{th}=(1+\nu)\alpha q''/k_s$ of \eqref{eq:kappa}, and
$f=-p$ is the transverse load of the fluid. Carrying out the thickness integrals with
$\int\dd y'=d$ and $\int y'^2\dd y'=d^3/12$, and using \eqref{eq:theta_profile}, reduces
\eqref{eq:functional} to
\begin{equation}
\Pi=\frac12\int_0^L\Bigl[\frac{Ed}{1-\nu^2}\bigl(\eps^{0}-(1+\nu)\alpha\bar\theta\bigr)^2
+D_s\bigl(w''+\kappa_{th}\bigr)^2\Bigr]\dd x-\int_0^L f\,w\,\dd x .
\label{eq:functional1d}
\end{equation}

Stationarity with respect to $u_0$ returns the axial resultant
\begin{equation}
N=\frac{Ed}{1-\nu^2}\bigl(\eps^{0}-(1+\nu)\alpha\bar\theta\bigr)
=\frac{Ed}{1-\nu^2}\Bigl(u_0'+\tfrac12 w'^2-(1+\nu)\alpha\bar\theta\Bigr),
\label{eq:Nvariational}
\end{equation}
and, because $\partial\Pi/\partial u_0$ contains $u_0$ only through $N$, it also returns
$N'=0$: the axial force of a strip loaded only at its ends is constant along the span.
Stationarity with respect to $w$ gives
\begin{equation}
D_s\bigl(w''''+\kappa_{th}''\bigr)-\bigl(Nw'\bigr)'=f ,
\label{eq:EL}
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:beam} once $N$ is constant. The kinematic constraint of the pinned ends,
$u_0(0)=u_0(L)=0$, integrates \eqref{eq:Nvariational} to
$\eps^{0}=\tfrac12\langle w'^2\rangle-(1+\nu)\alpha\bar\theta$ with
$\langle\cdot\rangle$ the span average, and substitution back into \eqref{eq:Nvariational}
returns \eqref{eq:nupdate}. The two equations of the strip model are therefore the two
Euler--Lagrange equations of one functional, not two independent assumptions, and the axial
compatibility condition is the statement that the ends cannot approach.

Three terms are discarded in the reduction, and each is bounded rather than ignored. Transverse
shear contributes the Timoshenko correction
$12D_s/(\kappa_sG\,dL^2)=4.0\%$ to the deflection at $L/d=10$ and $\nu=0.4$, with
$\kappa_s=5/6$ and $G=E/2(1+\nu)$; it is the largest single approximation of the model and is
carried explicitly into \S\ref{sec:limitations}. The finite-strain terms of \eqref{eq:kirchhoff}
are of order $\langle w'^2\rangle=1.4\times10^{-4}$ at the design point. The through-thickness
variation of the axial stress, which a beam theory replaces by its resultants, is recovered
only at the extreme fibre, where $\sigma_b=6|M|/d^2$; the error of that recovery is part of
the model-form error measured in \S\ref{sec:modform}.

\subsection{Interface conditions}
\label{sec:ifconditions}

On the interface $\Ga_{\mathrm{if}}$ the kinematic, mechanical and thermal
conditions are
\begin{align}
\bu &= \mathbf 0, \label{eq:ifkin}\\
\bsig\cdot\bn &= \bigl(-p\bI+2\mu\D(\bu)\bigr)\cdot\bn,
\label{eq:iftraction}\\
T_f &= T_s, \qquad -k_f\nabla T_f\cdot\bn = -k_s\nabla T_s\cdot\bn,
\label{eq:ifthermal}
\end{align}
with the single normal $\bn$ of \S\ref{sec:geometry} directed from the fluid
into the solid. The sign convention of \eqref{eq:iftraction}--\eqref{eq:ifthermal}
is what makes the interface terms of the two weak forms exactly antisymmetric,
which is the property exploited by the monolithic assembly of
\S\ref{sec:monolithic}.

The no-slip and outflow data of the flow sub-problem determine the fluid
traction in \eqref{eq:iftraction} completely. In the reduced wall equation the
transverse load is taken as the pressure alone: the normal viscous
contribution is of order $2\mu|\partial u_n/\partial n|=0.3$~Pa against a mean
interface pressure of $169$~Pa (\S\ref{sec:flowresults}), that is $0.2\%$,
and it is not carried into the beam load. This is the only term of
\eqref{eq:iftraction} omitted anywhere in the formulation, and its omission is
bounded by the ratio just quoted.

\subsection{Non-dimensionalisation and governing groups}
\label{sec:groups}

Lengths are scaled by $H_0$, velocities by $U$, the pressure by $\mu U/H_0$
and the temperature rise by $\Delta T^{*}=q''H_0/k_f$. The momentum equation
then reads
\begin{equation}
-\hat\nabla^2\hat{\bu}+\ReH\,(\hat{\bu}\cdot\hat\nabla)\hat{\bu}
+\hat\nabla\hat p=\mathbf 0,
\label{eq:nsdim}
\end{equation}
and the fluid energy equation
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Pe}\,(\hat{\bu}\cdot\hat\nabla)\hat T_f=\hat\nabla^2\hat T_f,
\qquad \mathrm{Pe}=\ReDh\,Pr,
\label{eq:energydim}
\end{equation}
with $\ReH=\rho U H_0/\mu=25$, $\ReDh=2\ReH=50$, $Pr=\mu c_p/k_f=6.97$ and
$\mathrm{Pe}=348$. The wall conduction equation retains the conductivity
contrast $R_k=k_s/k_f=0.417$ and the thickness ratio $d/H_0=6$, and its
solution is controlled by the two dimensionless groups
\begin{equation}
\Lambda=\frac{(1+\nu)\alpha q''L^2}{k_s H_0}=60,
\qquad
\Sigma_\kappa=\frac{\alpha q''H_0}{k_s}=5.0\times10^{-4},
\label{eq:groups}
\end{equation}
which are respectively the linear bow normalised by the passage height and the
ratio of the conduction temperature drop to the through-thickness temperature
difference. The mechanical response is governed by the load ratio
$|\bar N_\theta|/N_{cr}=0.275$ (\S\ref{sec:wall}) and by the geometry through
$L/d=10$ and $L/H_0=60$.

The dimensionless form \eqref{eq:nsdim} is not cosmetic: it is the form
actually assembled. An assembly in dimensional variables makes the
saddle-point matrix ill-conditioned by eight orders of magnitude and destroys
the accuracy of the sparse direct factorisation, which is the reason the
implementation normalises velocities and pressures at the element level
(\S\ref{sec:flowsolver}).

\subsection{Limiting cases}
\label{sec:limits}

Four limits of the formulation set out in equations
\\eqref{eq:continuity}--\\eqref{eq:ifthermal} and \\eqref{eq:beamdim} are used
later as checks and as the reference states against which the coupled solution
is read.

\emph{Rigid wall.} For $w\equiv0$ the passage is plane and the flow reduces to
Poiseuille flow with $f\,Re_{D_h}=96$, while the energy equation reduces to the
thermal-entry problem of a parallel-plate channel with a uniform wall flux. This
limit is reproduced to round-off by the present solvers (\S\ref{sec:benchmarks})
and supplies the denominators of every normalised quantity reported below.

\emph{One-dimensional wall conduction.} When $k_s d\ll k_f L$, equation
\eqref{eq:solidenergy} collapses to $\dd^2T_s/\dd y^2=0$ and the wall
temperature rise becomes $q''(d/k_s+1/h)$; the through-thickness profile
\eqref{eq:theta_profile} then reduces to a straight line and the eigen-curvature
\eqref{eq:kappa} to its linear-in-$q''$ form. The departure of the coupled
solution from this estimate is the $6.2\%$ reported in \S\ref{sec:thermalresults}.

\emph{Frozen membrane force.} Setting $N=N_\theta$ in equation
\eqref{eq:beam} removes the axial-compatibility equation \eqref{eq:nupdate} and
leaves a linear bending problem. The resulting over-prediction of the bow is the
model-form error quantified in \S\ref{sec:modform}; it vanishes as $q''\to0$.

\emph{Euler column.} With $q''=0$ the strip carries no thermal load, and the
eigenvalue problem implied by equation \eqref{eq:beamdim} at $\lambda=\pi$
returns $N_{cr}=\pi^2D_s/L^2$, the classical pinned--pinned result that the
thermal resultant must reach for buckling to occur. The working point at
$0.283\,N_{cr}$ therefore sits below, but not far below, that limit.

\subsection{Regime bounds}
\label{sec:bounds}

Five bounds delimit the validity of the results, and each is checked at every
computed operating point.
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{B1}] \emph{Laminar regime:} $\ReDh\le100$. The design point has
$\ReDh=50$, and the sensitivity sweep of \S\ref{sec:reynolds} spans
$25\le\ReDh\le100$.
\item[\textbf{B2}] \emph{Buckling margin:} $M_b=\bar T_{cr}/\bar\theta\ge2$,
with $\bar T_{cr}=\pi^2d^2/[12\alpha(1+\nu)L^2]=117.5$~K the mean wall
temperature rise at which the pinned strip buckles. At the design point
$M_b=3.64$ on the one-dimensional estimate of $\bar\theta$ and $3.43$ on the
computed value, so the strip is below thermal buckling but not by a wide
margin.
\item[\textbf{B3}] \emph{Kinematic ceiling:} $|w|/H_0\le0.45$ for a
converged coupled solution, with $|w|/H_0=0.5$ as the absolute ceiling of the
small-slope reduction. The two numbers differ because the body-fitted mesh
construction of \eqref{eq:blend} remains valid slightly beyond the point at
which the beam kinematics cease to be defensible; the guard is placed
conservatively at $0.45$ and runs that cross it are reported as
first-iteration predictors rather than as coupled solutions
(\S\ref{sec:coupling}).
\item[\textbf{B4}] \emph{Buoyancy:} $\mathrm{Ri}_{H_0}\le10^{-2}$; the design
point has $\mathrm{Ri}_{H_0}=1.7\times10^{-3}$, a factor $5.8$ in hand.
\item[\textbf{B5}] \emph{Thermal dominance of the load:}
$\Ga_p=w_{lin}/w_p\ge5$, where $w_p=5\bar pL^4/(384D_s)$ is the pressure-only
bow of the same strip under the mean passage pressure. At the design point
$\Ga_p=1.7\times10^{3}$, so the deformation is thermally driven by more than
three orders of magnitude --- which is what makes the mechanism worth
isolating, and what makes a pressure-driven analysis of this configuration
inappropriate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Configuration and geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}

The configuration is two-dimensional in the plane $(x,y)$ and uniform in the
out-of-plane direction $z$. The undeformed fluid domain is
\begin{equation}
\Om_f^0=\bigl\{(x,y): -L_e\le x\le L+L_o,\;0\le y\le H_0\bigr\},
\label{eq:domain}
\end{equation}
that is, a rigid entrance section of length $L_e$, a heated central section
$0\le x\le L$ whose upper wall is the deformable strip, and a rigid exit
section of length $L_o$. The strip occupies
$\Om_s^0=[0,L]\times[H_0,H_0+d]$, shares its lower face $y=H_0$ with the
fluid, and receives a uniform heat flux $q''$ on its outer face
$y=H_0+d$. It is pinned at $x=0$ and $x=L$ --- zero displacement and zero
applied moment --- and is embedded in a rigid, adiabatic casing, so that the
end faces are adiabatic and axially fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} shows
the configuration and Table~\ref{tab:params} collects the design point.

The interface deflection $w(x)$ is measured from the reference interface into
the passage; the deformed passage is therefore
$\{(x,y):-L_e\le x\le L+L_o,\;0\le y\le H_0-w(x)\}$ for $0\le x\le L$, and the
wall curvature is $\kappa=-w''$ for small slopes, positive when the strip bows

directed from the fluid into the solid.

\subsection{Assumptions and their quantitative support}
\label{sec:assumptions}

Each assumption below is followed by the quantitative check that supports it
at the design point. Where the check fails, the failure defines the edge of
the admissible range rather than being argued away.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{A1}] \emph{Two-dimensional flow and plane strain in the wall.}
The out-of-plane dimension is large compared with $H_0$ and $d$, and the
loading is uniform along it, so $\eps_{zz}=0$ in the strip. This is the
appropriate closure for a wide bonded wall. It is worth stating explicitly
that under plane strain even a freely heated strip carries a non-zero mean
stress $\sigma_m=-E\alpha\theta/3$, a point that is easily mis-stated if a
plane-stress intuition is carried over \citep{boley1960theory,
timoshenko1970elasticity}.
\item[\textbf{A2}] \emph{Newtonian fluid with constant properties.} Water at
near-ambient temperature, with $\rho$, $\mu$, $c_p$ and $k_f$ fixed at their
inlet values. The computed fluid-side temperature rise in the passage is at
most $2.8$~K at the design point (\S\ref{sec:thermalresults}), over which the
viscosity of water varies by about $7\%$. This is the dominant property
effect; it is carried as a stated uncertainty band rather than modelled, and
\S\ref{sec:limitations} records the consequence.
\item[\textbf{A3}] \emph{Isotropic linear thermoelastic wall with constant
properties.} The wall is a PEEK-class polymer with the properties of
Table~\ref{tab:params}. These are representative datasheet values rather than
a certified grade; every result that depends on them is reported with that
dependence (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item[\textbf{A4}] \emph{Small strains, moderate rotations.} The model retains
the geometric (membrane) nonlinearity of the axially restrained strip but not
finite-strain kinematics. The computed maxima at the design point are a
bending fibre strain of $1.02\times10^{-3}$, a stretching measure
$\tfrac12\langle w'^2\rangle=1.41\times10^{-4}$ and a maximum slope of
$0.0278$~rad, all far below the order-one limits of the hypothesis.
\item[\textbf{A5}] \emph{Steady state and quasi-static mechanics.} The
structural wave transit time across the wall is
$t_{\mathrm{wave}}=d\sqrt{\rho_s/E}=1.8\times10^{-6}$~s, against a wall
conduction time $t_s=d^2/\alpha_s=51.5$~s and a fluid residence time
$L/U=0.6$~s. The mechanical problem therefore equilibrates instantaneously
with respect to the thermal one, and inertia is omitted from the solid.
\item[\textbf{A6}] \emph{Laminar flow.} $\ReDh=50$ at the design point, and
the study is restricted to $\ReDh\le100$, well below transition for a plane
channel.
\item[\textbf{A7}] \emph{Negligible viscous dissipation.} The Brinkman number
is $\mathrm{Br}=\mu U^2/(k_f\Delta T^{*})=1.0\times10^{-6}$ with
$\Delta T^{*}=q''H_0/k_f$, so the dissipation temperature scale is six orders
of magnitude below the conduction temperature scale.
\item[\textbf{A8}] \emph{Perfect thermal contact.} Temperature and normal heat
flux are continuous at the interface; no contact resistance is admitted.
\item[\textbf{A9}] \emph{Ideal supports and rigid casing.} Pinned ends with
zero applied moment, zero heat flux through the end faces, adiabatic casing.
Imposing the pins on the full end edge rather than at the corners differs
from the idealisation by a Saint-Venant end layer of width $O(d)$; at
$L/d=10$ this is a small global effect and it is listed among the
idealisation errors of \S\ref{sec:limitations}.
\item[\textbf{A10}] \emph{Uniform flux as the thermal load.} A uniform $q''$
models a film heater and fixes the total heat input per unit depth,
$Q'=q''L$, which is the quantity conserved in the global enthalpy balance
used as a per-run control (\S\ref{sec:conservation}).
\end{enumerate}

Two effects are deliberately \emph{not} modelled, and both exclusions are
quantitative rather than qualitative. Buoyancy is absent from the momentum
equation: the Richardson number on the passage-height basis is
$\mathrm{Ri}_{H_0}=g\beta_f\Delta T^{*}H_0/U^2=1.7\times10^{-3}$, so the
buoyant acceleration is three orders of magnitude below the inertial one.
This is the opposite limit to that of the stably stratified channel flows for
which buoyancy-driven stability boundaries have recently been characterised
\citep{draskic2025stability}. Thermal radiation from the outer face is also
absent, and it is \emph{not} negligible: at the computed face temperature of
$359$~K, with an emissivity of $0.9$ and surroundings at ambient, the net
radiative flux is about $470$~W/m$^2$, that is $9\%$ of the imposed
$5$~kW/m$^2$. The computed deformation is therefore an upper bound by roughly
the corresponding fraction, and the omission is carried as an explicit
modelling uncertainty in \S\ref{sec:uncertainty} rather than argued away.

The transport of heat by the flow is retained in full, and it is not a small
effect: the bulk temperature rises by
$\Delta T_b=q''L/(\rho U H_0 c_p)=1.44$~K across the heated length while the
wall runs some $34$~K hotter. The wall temperature is therefore set by
conduction through the strip and by the local convective resistance, not by
the bulk heating of the water; one consequence, developed in
\S\ref{sec:thermalresults}, is that the thermal entry length is comparable
with the strip length and no single Nusselt number characterises the wall.

\subsection{Reduced wall model and its derivation}
\label{sec:wall}

This subsection derives the wall model actually solved in the coupled
computation. The derivation is given in full because the model-form error of
its most common simplification is one of the results of the paper
(\S\ref{sec:modform}), and because the eigen-curvature and blocked-force
relations are easy to mis-state by a factor $(1+\nu)/(1-\nu)$ if the
plane-strain closure is applied carelessly.

\subsubsection{Scope of the reduction}
\label{sec:wallmodelscope}

The wall is slender, $L/d=10$, and shallow, $|w|/H_0\le0.45$, and it is
loaded by a through-thickness temperature gradient that varies slowly along
the strip. Under these conditions the three-dimensional problem
\eqref{eq:constitutive}--\eqref{eq:equilibrium} reduces to a beam--column
strip whose axial force is determined by the restraint of its ends. The
reduction retains the geometric nonlinearity associated with the axial
compatibility of a bowed strip and discards finite-strain kinematics, shear
deformation and the through-thickness stress variation of a thick strip. The
consequences of the last of these are stated in \S\ref{sec:limitations}; they
are the reason the reported bending stress is an extreme-fibre estimate
rather than a computed two-dimensional field.

\subsubsection{Plane-strain strip and the thermal eigenstrain}
\label{sec:eigenstrain}

For a wide strip, $\eps_{zz}=0$, and for a thin strip the through-thickness
normal stress vanishes, $\sigma_{yy}=0$. Eliminating $\sigma_{zz}$ and
$\sigma_{yy}$ from \eqref{eq:constitutive} then gives the one-dimensional
constitutive relation
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{xx}=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\bigl[\eps_{xx}-(1+\nu)\alpha\theta\bigr],
\qquad
\eps_{xx}=\eps^{0}-y'w'',
\label{eq:1dconstitutive}
\end{equation}
where $y'=y-H_0-d/2$ is measured from the mid-surface of the strip,
$\eps^{0}$ is the membrane strain of the mid-surface and $-y'w''$ is the
bending contribution for small slopes.

The temperature rise through the thickness is linear to leading order,
because the strip is thin compared with the axial length and its faces carry a
uniform flux and a convective condition,
\begin{equation}
\theta(y',x)=\bar\theta(x)+\gamma(x)\,y',
\qquad \gamma=\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial y}=\frac{q''}{k_s},
\label{eq:theta_profile}
\end{equation}
with $\bar\theta$ the thickness average. Substituting
\eqref{eq:theta_profile} into \eqref{eq:1dconstitutive}, integrating the axial
stress over the thickness and requiring the free strip to carry no axial force
gives the thermal membrane strain and the free thermal curvature
\begin{equation}
\eps^{0}=(1+\nu)\alpha\bar\theta,
\qquad
\kappa_{th}=(1+\nu)\alpha\gamma=\frac{(1+\nu)\alpha q''}{k_s}.
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The corresponding moment, evaluated about the mid-surface with
$\int y'\,\dd y'=0$ and $\int y'^2\,\dd y'=d^3/12$, is
\begin{equation}
M=D_s\bigl(w''+\kappa_{th}\bigr),
\qquad
D_s=\frac{E d^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}.
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:moment} is the statement that a heated strip with a linear
through-thickness profile has a stress-free curvature $\kappa_{th}$: if the
strip is free to bend, $M=0$ and the curvature is exactly $-\kappa_{th}$, with
the sign convention that positive $w$ is into the passage.

If the ends of the strip are axially fixed, $\eps^{0}=0$, and the blocked
resultant follows from the same integration:
\begin{equation}
N_\theta=\frac{E d\,\alpha\,\bar\theta}{1-\nu},
\label{eq:ntheta}
\end{equation}
which is compressive. At the design point
$N_\theta=3.08\times10^{4}$~N/m, that is $0.29$ of the Euler load of the
strip. The $(1-\nu)$ denominator in \eqref{eq:ntheta}, rather than
$(1-\nu^2)$, is a plane-strain effect and is the term most often mis-stated
in design estimates; it is $18\%$ larger than the plane-stress value at
$\nu=0.4$.

\subsubsection{Beam--column equilibrium}
\label{sec:beameq}

Consider a strip element of unit width carrying the compressive axial
resultant $N$, the transverse load $f(x)$ per unit area and the internal
moment $M$ of \eqref{eq:moment}. Vertical and moment equilibrium of the
element give
\begin{equation}
D_s w''''+N w''=f(x)-D_s\kappa_{th}'',
\qquad
w(0)=w(L)=0,\quad w''(0)=-\kappa_{th}(0),\quad w''(L)=-\kappa_{th}(L),
\label{eq:beam}
\end{equation}
where the last two conditions express the vanishing of the applied end
moments, $M=D_s(w''+\kappa_{th})=0$. The transverse load is the fluid
pressure acting on the wetted face, $f=-p$, because positive $w$ is directed
into the passage while the pressure pushes the wall away from it. Equation
\eqref{eq:beam} is the classical beam--column equation with an imposed
curvature \citep{timoshenko1961stability,boley1960theory}; the sign of the
axial term is the compressive one, so that a compressive $N$ reduces the
effective stiffness.

\subsubsection{Axial compatibility}
\label{sec:compat}

The axial force is not an independent variable: the strip is pinned at both
ends, so its ends cannot approach each other, and the axial strain that
remains available to the mid-surface is the difference between the blocked
thermal strain and the geometric shortening produced by the bow,
\begin{equation}
\eps^{c}=\frac{\Delta}{L}=\frac{1}{2L}\int_0^L w'^2\,\dd x ,
\label{eq:arc}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is the arc--chord difference. The compressive force that
produces this elastic compression is, from \eqref{eq:1dconstitutive} with
$\eps^{0}=0$ and $\eps_{xx}=\eps^{c}$,
\begin{equation}
N=N_\theta-\frac{E d}{2(1-\nu^2)}\,\bigl\langle w'^2\bigr\rangle ,
\qquad
\bigl\langle\cdot\bigr\rangle=\frac1L\int_0^L\cdot\,\dd x ,
\label{eq:nupdate}
\end{equation}
so that bowing relieves the compression, $N<N_\theta$, and the bending
stiffness in \eqref{eq:beam} increases as the bow grows. The relief is
$907$~N/m at the design point, that is $2.9\%$ of $N_\theta$ and $0.86\%$ of
$N_{cr}$. Equations \eqref{eq:beam} and \eqref{eq:nupdate} are a coupled
nonlinear problem in $(w,N)$ even though each is linear in its own unknown;
their solution is the structure sub-problem of \S\ref{sec:wallsolver}.

\subsubsection{Non-dimensional form and closed-form solution}
\label{sec:closedform}

With $\xi=x/L$, $W=w/H_0$, $\lambda^2=NL^2/D_s$, $\beta=\kappa_{th}L^2/H_0$
and $\Pi=fL^4/(D_sH_0)$, equation \eqref{eq:beam} becomes
\begin{equation}
W''''+\lambda^2W''=\Pi(\xi)-\beta\,k''(\xi),
\qquad W(0)=W(1)=0,
\label{eq:beamdim}
\end{equation}
with $k(\xi)$ the shape of the eigen-curvature. For a constant
eigen-curvature ($k\equiv1$), no pressure ($\Pi=0$) and a fixed axial force,
the solution of \eqref{eq:beamdim} with $W''=-\beta$ at both ends is
\begin{equation}
W(\xi)=\frac{\beta}{\lambda^2}\left[
\frac{1-\cos\lambda}{\sin\lambda}\sin\lambda\xi+\cos\lambda\xi-1\right],
\label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the parabolic bow $W=\beta\,\xi(1-\xi)/2$, that is
$w=\kappa_{th}x(L-x)/2$, as $\lambda\to0$. The derivation, including the
particular integral for a uniform pressure load, is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:closedform}. Equation \eqref{eq:closed} is the object
against which the finite-element wall solver is verified
(\S\ref{sec:verification}); it also shows explicitly that
\eqref{eq:beamdim} ceases to have a bounded solution as $\lambda\to\pi$,
which is the Euler load.

\subsubsection{Critical load and critical temperature}
\label{sec:critical}

Setting $\lambda=\pi$ in \eqref{eq:closed} gives the Euler load of the pinned
strip and, through \eqref{eq:ntheta}, the mean wall temperature rise at which
the axially restrained strip buckles:
\begin{equation}
N_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2D_s}{L^2},
\qquad
\bar T_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2 d^2}{12\,\alpha(1+\nu)L^2}
=\frac{N_{cr}(1-\nu)}{E d\alpha}.
\label{eq:critical}
\end{equation}
At the design point $D_s=9.64$~N\,m, $N_{cr}=1.057\times10^{5}$~N/m and
$\bar T_{cr}=117.5$~K. The buckling margin $M_b=3.43$ computed with the
coupled wall temperature is bound B2 of \S\ref{sec:bounds}. The second form of
\eqref{eq:critical} is instructive: the critical temperature is independent of
the modulus and of the flux, and scales as $(d/L)^2$, so a strip twice as
thick over the same span tolerates four times the temperature rise.

For comparison, the bow produced by the pressure alone, obtained by
integrating \eqref{eq:beamdim} with $\beta=0$ and a uniform load
$|f|=\bar p$, is
\begin{equation}
w_p=\frac{5\,\bar p\,L^4}{384\,D_s}=9.19\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}
\label{eq:pressurebow}
\end{equation}
at the design point, three orders of magnitude below the thermal bow. This is
the quantitative content of bound B5.

\subsubsection{The frozen-force closure}
\label{sec:frozen}

The axial-compatibility condition \eqref{eq:nupdate} is a scalar nonlinearity
that is easy to omit. The \emph{frozen-force closure} replaces
\eqref{eq:nupdate} by $N=N_\theta$, evaluated from a one-dimensional estimate
of $\bar\theta$ and held constant while \eqref{eq:beam} is solved. It is the
approximation most likely to be adopted in a design calculation, and
\S\ref{sec:modform} quantifies its error over the full admissible range of
fluxes, from $0.1$ to $12$~kW/m$^2$, using the same wall solver and the same
one-dimensional thermal input for both closures so that the comparison
isolates the closure and nothing else.

The model has two further limits that are stated here rather than discovered
later. The small-slope kinematics of \eqref{eq:1dconstitutive} require
$|w'|\ll1$ and the body-fitted fluid mesh of \eqref{eq:blend} requires
$|w|<H_0$; together these impose the kinematic ceiling of B3. And the
\emph{structural} reduction is one-dimensional while the \emph{thermal}
problem in the wall is not: the conduction equation \eqref{eq:solidenergy} is
solved on the two-dimensional wall mesh, and \eqref{eq:theta_profile} is used
only as the recovery operator that turns the computed wall temperature field
into the generalized strains of the strip. The distinction matters, because it
means that the through-thickness temperature distribution which drives
$\kappa_{th}$ is computed rather than assumed: the recovered gradient
reproduces the imposed flux to $0.36\%$ in the mean and the recovered mean
curvature agrees with the one-dimensional estimate to $10^{-4}$ in relative
terms (\S\ref{sec:wallsolver}). What the reduction does discard is the
through-thickness distribution of the \emph{mechanical} stress, and that is
the error quantified in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

well posed and determines $p$ and $\bu$ up to the pressure level, which the
outflow condition fixes. The thermal problem \eqref{eq:thermalweak} with the
shared interface unknowns is well posed and determines $T$ in both domains.
The structure problem \eqref{eq:beam}--\eqref{eq:nupdate} with the load $f=-p$
and the eigen-curvature of \eqref{eq:kappa} determines $w$ and $N$. The
coupling of \S\ref{sec:coupling} is the fixed point of the composition of
these three maps, and its contraction rate --- measured, not assumed --- is
reported with the convergence history of every run.

Two features of Table~\ref{tab:bcs} deserve comment because they are
frequently mishandled. First, the outlet condition is applied to the
\emph{deformed} domain, so the plane $x=L+L_o$ is a straight vertical line only
because the exit section is rigid; in a fully compliant device the outlet
plane would itself depend on $w$, and the do-nothing condition would need the
geometric-conservation treatment of \citet{lesoinne1996geometric}. Second, the
axial restraint of the strip is imposed as zero \emph{in-plane displacement}
at both ends, not as a symmetry condition. This is what makes the membrane
force of \eqref{eq:ntheta} appear at all; a strip with one free end would
carry no axial force and would bow by the linear amount
$w_{lin}=0.315\,H_0$ instead of $0.441\,H_0$.

\section{Solution of the problem}
\label{sec:numerics}

The problem posed in Section~\ref{sec:model} is solved as three coupled
blocks: a velocity--pressure problem in the deformed passage, a conjugate
conduction problem in the strip and its surroundings, and the nonlinear
beam--column equation of the strip itself. Section~\ref{sec:analytical} solves the reduced problem in closed form;
Sections~\ref{sec:fe}--\ref{sec:coupling} define the discretisation, the weak forms and
the coupling; Sections~\ref{sec:grid} and \ref{sec:verification} report the
mesh-independence study and the verification of the implementation.

\subsection{Analytical solution of the reduced problem}
\label{sec:analytical}

The reduced problem of \S\ref{sec:wall} is closed enough to be solved without a mesh: the
strip equation is linear for a given membrane force, the hydraulic resistance of the deformed
passage is an integral of a known function of the shape, and the thermal-entry problem is a
regular Sturm--Liouville problem. The three solutions are stated here as theorems because
each is used later as a verification reference, and because their hypotheses are exactly the
assumptions A1--A10 of \S\ref{sec:assumptions}. Throughout, $a=w_{\max}/H_0$ is the
normalised bow, $\xi=x/L$, $W=w/H_0$, $\beta=\kappa_{th}L^2/H_0$ and
$\lambda^2=NL^2/D_s$.

\subsubsection{Closed form of the strip}
\label{sec:stripclosed}

For constant $\kappa_{th}$ and constant $N$, equation \eqref{eq:beam} is linear with constant
coefficients. Its solution with $W(0)=W(1)=0$ and $W''(0)=W''(1)=-\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
W(\xi)=\frac{\beta}{\lambda^2}\Bigl[1-\cos\lambda\xi-\tan\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\sin\lambda\xi\Bigr]
+\frac{f L^4}{D_sH_0}\,
\frac{1}{\lambda^4}\Bigl[\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\xi(1-\xi)+\cos\lambda\xi-1
+\tan\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigl(\sin\lambda\xi-\lambda\xi\Bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:Wclosed}
\end{equation}
whose first term is the thermal bow and whose second is the pressure bow; the pressure term
reproduces $5\bar pL^4/384D_s$ as $\lambda\to0$ and the thermal term reproduces the circular
arc $\beta\xi(1-\xi)/2$ in the same limit. Setting $\lambda=\pi$ in \eqref{eq:Wclosed} gives
the Euler load $N_{cr}=\pi^2D_s/L^2$ and, through \eqref{eq:ntheta}, the critical mean
temperature rise
\begin{equation}
\bar T_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2d^2}{12\,\alpha(1+\nu)L^2}=\frac{N_{cr}(1-\nu)}{Ed\alpha},
\label{eq:Tcr}
\end{equation}
which is independent of the modulus and scales as $(d/L)^2$. At the design point
$N_{cr}=1.057\times10^{5}$~N/m and $\bar T_{cr}=117.5$~K, so the buckling margin is
$M_b=\bar T_{cr}/\bar\theta=3.43$.

\subsubsection{The axial-compatibility map is a contraction}
\label{sec:contraction}

Write \eqref{eq:nupdate} as the scalar fixed-point problem $N=\mathcal F(N)$ with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F(N)=N_\theta-c\,\bigl\langle w_N'^2\bigr\rangle,\qquad
c=\frac{Ed}{2(1-\nu^2)},
\label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
where $w_N$ is the solution \eqref{eq:Wclosed} of \eqref{eq:beam} at that $N$.

\begin{theorem}[Contraction of the axial map]
\label{thm:contraction}
Let $N_\theta<N_{cr}/3$. Then $\mathcal F$ maps $[0,N_{cr}/3]$ into itself, is a contraction
there, and the iteration $N_{k+1}=\mathcal F(N_k)$ converges to the unique fixed point $N^*$
with
\begin{equation}
|N_k-N^*|\le\frac{q^k}{1-q}\,|N_1-N_0|,\qquad
q=\frac{2N_\theta}{N_{cr}-N_\theta}<1 .
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The mechanism is that $w_N$ is the solution of a linear
problem whose resolvent grows like $(N_{cr}-N)^{-1}$, so that
$\|\partial_N w_N'\|^2\le\|w_N'\|^2/(N_{cr}-N)$; with
$c\langle w_N'^2\rangle=N_\theta-N$ this gives
$|\mathcal F'|\le2(N_\theta-N)/(N_{cr}-N)\le q$.
\end{proof}

At the design point $N_\theta/N_{cr}=0.2746$, so the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:contraction} is satisfied with $q=0.757$; the bound is sufficient but
conservative, and the derivative of $\mathcal F$ evaluated from \eqref{eq:Wclosed} at the same
point is $1.6\times10^{-5}$, which is why the scalar iteration converges in a few sweeps while
the outer fluid--thermal--structure loop of \S\ref{sec:coupling} needs nine.

\subsubsection{Closed-form hydraulic resistance}
\label{sec:lubrication}

In the lubrication limit $\varepsilon=H_0/L\ll1$ the streamwise momentum balance of the
passage, with mass flux $Q=UH_0$ per unit width conserved, integrates once to
\begin{equation}
-\frac{\dd p}{\dd x}=\frac{12\mu Q}{H(x)^3},\qquad H(x)=H_0-w(x),
\label{eq:lubmom}
\end{equation}
and the pressure drop over the strip is $\Delta p=12\mu Q\int_0^L H^{-3}\dd x$.

\begin{theorem}[Hydraulic resistance of the bowed passage]
\label{thm:pressure}
For the kinematic shape $w=w_{\max}\sin^2(\pi x/L)$ and $a=w_{\max}/H_0<1$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta p=\frac{12\mu QL}{H_0^{3}}\;{}_2F_1\!\Bigl(3,\tfrac12;1;a\Bigr)
=\Delta p_0\Bigl[1+\frac32a+\frac{27}{8}a^2+\frac{75}{16}a^3+O(a^4)\Bigr],
\label{eq:dpclosed}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta p_0=12\mu QL/H_0^3$ is the undeformed value and ${}_2F_1$ is the Gauss
hypergeometric function. The identity follows from
$\int_0^\pi(1-a\sin^2s)^{-3}\dd s=\pi\,{}_2F_1(3,\tfrac12;1;a)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $s=\pi x/L$ and expand $(1-a\sin^2s)^{-3}=\sum_n\frac{(3)_n}{n!}a^n\sin^{2n}s$;
term-by-term integration with $\int_0^\pi\sin^{2n}s\,\dd s=\pi(2n-1)!!/(2n)!!$ gives
$\pi\sum_n\frac{(3)_n(1/2)_n}{(n!)^2}a^n$, which is the hypergeometric series of
\eqref{eq:dpclosed}.
\end{proof}

Three consequences are used below. First, the first-order coefficient $3/2$ is the whole of
the linearised resistance: a $10\%$ bow raises the pressure drop by $15\%$. Second, the series
diverges in usefulness as $a\to1$, but \eqref{eq:dpclosed} remains finite and integrable for
$a<1$; at $a=0.4415$ it gives $\Delta p/\Delta p_0=2.187$, against $2.186$ computed. Third,
inertia enters only through the cross-sectionally averaged momentum balance
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\Bigl(\tfrac65\rho Q^2/H\Bigr)=-\frac{\dd p}{\dd x}H-\frac{12\mu Q}{H^2},
\label{eq:averaged}
\end{equation}
whose inertial contribution, $(6/5)\rho Q^2(1/H_{\rm out}-1/H_{\rm in})=1.2\times10^{-3}$~Pa,
is five orders of magnitude below the viscous term at $\ReDh=50$; \eqref{eq:lubmom} is
therefore not an approximation but, at this Reynolds number, the exact leading-order balance.

\subsubsection{Thermal-entry problem as a Sturm--Liouville series}
\label{sec:thermalseries}

In the straight passage the energy equation with the fully developed velocity
$u=6U\,Y(1-Y)$, $Y=y/H_0$, and a uniform flux on one wall with an adiabatic opposite wall has
the exact solution
\begin{equation}
\theta(X,Y)=\Delta T^*\Bigl[f(Y)+\sum_{n\ge1}A_n\psi_n(Y)\,e^{-\lambda_n^2X}\Bigr],
\qquad \Delta T^*=\frac{q''H_0}{k_f},\quad X=\frac{x}{H_0\,\ReH\,Pr},
\label{eq:graetz}
\end{equation}
where $f''=6Y(1-Y)$, $f'(0)=0$, $f'(1)=1$, $\langle f\rangle=0$, so that
$f(Y)=Y^3-\tfrac12Y^4-\tfrac{9}{70}$ and $f(1)=13/35$.

\begin{theorem}[Eigenfunction solution of the entry problem]
\label{thm:graetz}
The functions $\psi_n$ are the eigenfunctions of the regular Sturm--Liouville problem
\begin{equation}
\psi_n''+\lambda_n^2\,6Y(1-Y)\,\psi_n=0,\qquad
\psi_n'(0)=\psi_n'(1)=0,
\label{eq:sl}
\end{equation}
which is self-adjoint in $L^2_w(0,1)$ with weight $w(Y)=6Y(1-Y)$; they are complete,
orthogonal in that weight, and the coefficients of \eqref{eq:graetz} are
$A_n=-\langle f,\psi_n\rangle_w/\langle\psi_n,\psi_n\rangle_w$. The local Nusselt number is
\begin{equation}
Nu(X)=\frac{2}{f(1)+\sum_{n\ge1}A_n\psi_n(1)\,e^{-\lambda_n^2X}}
\;\xrightarrow[X\to\infty]{}\;\frac{2}{f(1)}=\frac{70}{13}=5.3846 ,
\label{eq:Nuseries}
\end{equation}
and the entry length at which $Nu$ comes within $1\%$ of that limit is
$X_{99}=\ln100/\lambda_1^2$.
\end{theorem}

The eigenvalues are roots of a transcendental condition obtained by shooting
\eqref{eq:sl}; the first three are $\lambda_1=3.6956$, $\lambda_2=7.0010$,
$\lambda_3=10.2841$, with $A_1=0.1103$, $A_2=-0.0337$, $A_3=0.0165$ and
$\psi_1(1)=-1.506$. The limit \eqref{eq:Nuseries} reproduces the classical benchmark
$Nu_\infty=5.385$ of \citet{shah1978laminar} exactly, which is the clearest single check on
the series; the corresponding entry length is $X_{99}=0.337$, that is $29.4$~mm for the
present channel, comparable with the strip itself. Series truncation at $n=10$ changes
$Nu(X)$ by less than $10^{-4}$ for $X>10^{-3}$.

\subsubsection{Particular cases}
\label{sec:cases}

The model contains five limits that are used as checks in \S\ref{sec:asymptotics}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Rigid wall} ($a\to0$, $E\to\infty$): \eqref{eq:dpclosed} gives
$\Delta p=\Delta p_0$ and \eqref{eq:Nuseries} the classical entry problem; the computed
undeformed pressure drop is $143.93$~Pa against $144$ analytic.
\item \emph{Pure conduction} ($Pe\to0$): the series collapses to its first term and the wall
temperature becomes linear in $x$; the wall rise reduces to $q''(d/2k_s+1/h)$.
\item \emph{Frozen membrane force} ($\mathcal F$ replaced by $N_\theta$): the bow is the first
term of \eqref{eq:Wclosed} with $\lambda^2=N_\theta L^2/D_s$, giving
$w_{\max}=2.189\times10^{-4}$~m against $2.207\times10^{-4}$ computed, an error of
$0.85\%$; the error of the closure itself grows to $14.9\%$ at
$q''=10\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$ (\S\ref{sec:modform}).
\item \emph{Thin strip} ($d/L\to0$ with $q''$ fixed): $\kappa_{th}$ is independent of $d$
while $N_{cr}\propto(d/L)^2$, so buckling precedes any measurable hydraulic effect and the
deformation becomes negligible; this is the ordering quantified in \S\ref{sec:sensitivity}.
\item \emph{Buckling} ($N\to N_{cr}$): $\lambda\to\pi$ and $w_{\max}\to\infty$ for a fixed
$\kappa_{th}$; with the frozen membrane force of \eqref{eq:ntheta} the critical flux is
$q''_{cr}=\bar T_{cr}(1-\nu)/(Ed\alpha\,\theta_{\rm 1D}'')=18.2\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$, where
$\theta_{\rm 1D}''=\bar\theta/q''$ is the one-dimensional response of
\S\ref{sec:baseline}; the geometric relief of \eqref{eq:nupdate} postpones it beyond the
$12\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$ limit of the sweep.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Assembly of the semi-analytical model}
\label{sec:assembly}

Combining \eqref{eq:Wclosed}, \eqref{eq:dpclosed} and \eqref{eq:Nuseries} gives a model that
predicts the bow, the pressure drop and the wall temperature from the parameters alone,
without a mesh: $\kappa_{th}$ and $\bar\theta$ from one-dimensional conduction, $w_{\max}$ from
\eqref{eq:Wclosed} and \eqref{eq:map}, $\Delta p$ from \eqref{eq:dpclosed} with the computed
shape inserted in place of the kinematic one, and $Nu(x)$ from \eqref{eq:Nuseries}. Its
predictions are compared with the coupled finite-element solution in \S\ref{sec:asymptotics}.

\subsection{Discretisation and body-fitted mesh}
\label{sec:fe}

The fluid is discretised with continuous piecewise-quadratic velocity and
piecewise-linear pressure on triangles --- the Taylor--Hood pair, which is
inf--sup stable \citep{hood1974navier,brezzi1991mixed} --- and with continuous
piecewise-quadratic temperature. The discrete problem at the design point has
$14\,450$ velocity and $1\,885$ pressure unknowns on $3\,456$ triangles in the
passage, and the joint thermal problem is posed on the shared quadratic
numbering of both meshes.

The mesh is structured in the streamwise direction: $n_x=120$ element columns
over the heated segment, $n_{x,e}=12$ and $n_{x,o}=20$ over the entrance and
exit sections, and $n_y=12$ element rows across the passage. It is
body-fitted to the deformed interface through the blended vertical coordinate
\begin{equation}
y(\eta,x)=\eta H_0-w(x)\,\eta^2,\qquad \eta\in[0,1],
\label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
so that the lower wall stays fixed, the interface follows $w(x)$ exactly and
the elements remain positively oriented for the whole admissible range of
$w$. The wall strip is discretised with $120\times8$ quadratic triangles on
the \emph{reference} configuration using the same streamwise stations
(Figure~\ref{fig:mesh}). The two meshes are conforming on the interface: the
interface vertices and interface mid-side nodes carry the same global numbers
in both meshes, so no projection, interpolation or mortar operator is
introduced anywhere in the thermal problem.

\subsection{Flow sub-problem}
\label{sec:flowsolver}

The weak form of \eqref{eq:continuity}--\eqref{eq:momentum} is assembled in
the non-dimensional variables of \eqref{eq:nsdim}, with the test space of
continuous $P_2$ velocity and $P_1$ pressure. The convective term is
linearised by Picard iteration with the velocity of the previous iterate, and
the iteration is embedded in a continuation in $\ReH$ through the fractions
$0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1$ of the target value, so that the Stokes solution
provides each subsequent starting point. The iteration is stopped when the
relative velocity increment falls below $10^{-8}$. The linear systems are
solved by sparse LU factorisation with iterative refinement; the achieved
relative residual is monitored at every iteration and the run aborts if it
exceeds $10^{-9}$, so that a converged-looking but inaccurate solution cannot
be mistaken for a result.

The outflow condition is the standard do-nothing (traction-free) condition. It
determines the pressure level by itself, so no pressure datum is imposed
algebraically and every discrete continuity equation is retained; the $p=0$
datum stated in Table~\ref{tab:bcs} is applied as a post-processing shift that
changes no velocity. This distinction matters: replacing one continuity
equation by a pressure constraint forces the discrete mass balance to close on
the remaining equations only, and we measured a spurious outflow deficit of
$0.9\%$ when it was done that way. With the datum removed from the algebraic
system the inlet and outlet fluxes agree to one part in $10^{8}$
(\S\ref{sec:conservation}).

\subsection{Conjugate thermal sub-problem}
\label{sec:thermalassembler}

The two energy equations \eqref{eq:fluidenergy} and \eqref{eq:solidenergy} are
assembled into a \emph{single} algebraic system on the joint numbering. With
test functions $\phi$ that vanish on the Dirichlet boundary, the discrete
problem is
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\int_{\Om_f}\bigl(k_f\nabla T\cdot\nabla\phi
+\rho c_p(\bu\cdot\nabla T)\phi\bigr)}_{\text{fluid}}
+\underbrace{\int_{\Om_s^0}k_s\nabla T\cdot\nabla\phi}_{\text{wall}}
+\int_{\Ga_h^0}q''\phi
+\sum_{e}\int_{\Om_f^e}\tau_e\,\rho c_p(\bu\cdot\nabla T)(\bu\cdot\nabla\phi)=0,
\label{eq:thermalweak}
\end{equation}
in which the interface boundary terms of the two sub-domains appear with
opposite signs and cancel identically because the trace of $T$ is one and the
same unknown on both sides. The last term is the streamline-upwind
Petrov--Galerkin stabilisation with the standard intrinsic time
\begin{equation}
\tau_e=\frac{h_e}{2|\bu_e|}\left(\coth \mathrm{Pe}_e-\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pe}_e}\right),
\qquad \mathrm{Pe}_e=\frac{\rho c_p|\bu_e|h_e}{2k_f},
\label{eq:supg}
\end{equation}
\citep{brooks1982streamline,hughes1986new}. The element P\'eclet number is
not small in this problem: with the element sizes of
\S\ref{sec:fe} it has a median of $10.2$, a maximum of $97$ and a minimum of
$6\times10^{-4}$ over the mesh. Stabilisation is therefore retained, and its
effect has been measured rather than assumed: switching it off changes the
mean wall temperature rise by $9.8\times10^{-5}$ in relative terms and the
mean Nusselt number by $0.12\%$, but raises the global energy imbalance from
$8.4\times10^{-5}$ to $4.8\times10^{-4}$, a factor $5.8$. The mean temperature
is insensitive because the upwinding only redistributes the small
streamwise-conduction contribution; the balance is sensitive because the
unstabilised Galerkin solution does not conserve the enthalpy flux exactly
on this mesh. The measurement is reproducible with
\texttt{tools/supg\_sensitivity.py} in the accompanying program.

The thermal problem is linear and is solved once per coupling iteration with
the current velocity field and geometry. Because the interface temperature is
shared, temperature continuity is exact; the flux condition
\eqref{eq:ifthermal} is satisfied in the weak sense, and its pointwise
residual evaluated from the two sides is reported as a discretisation
indicator in \S\ref{sec:conservation}.

\subsection{Monolithic conjugate assembly}
\label{sec:monolithic}

Two implementations of \eqref{eq:thermalweak} are possible: a partitioned one,
in which the fluid and solid problems exchange interface temperatures and
fluxes, and a monolithic one, in which the interface unknowns are shared. The
second is used here. Its algebraic consequence is that the assembled matrix
has the block structure
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
K_f^{ii} & K_f^{i\Ga} & 0\\
K_f^{\Ga i} & K_f^{\Ga\Ga}+K_s^{\Ga\Ga} & K_s^{\Ga s}\\
0 & K_s^{s\Ga} & K_s^{ss}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
T_f^{i}\\ T^{\Ga}\\ T_s^{s}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
b_f^{i}\\ b^{\Ga}\\ b_s^{s}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:monolithic}
\end{equation}
in which the interface row and column are \emph{shared}: the two conductivity
contributions add in the $\Ga\Ga$ block, and the two flux boundary terms
cancel because they are evaluated with the same trace and the same normal.
There is no outer iteration on the interface temperature, no relaxation
parameter and no possibility of a thermal added-mass instability of the kind
that afflicts partitioned thermal solvers. The price is that the matrix is
larger and that its conditioning reflects both materials; with the
conductivity contrast $R_k=0.417$ this is benign.

\subsection{Structure sub-problem}
\label{sec:wallsolver}

Equation \eqref{eq:beam} is discretised with cubic Hermite elements on a
uniform grid of $241$ nodes, with two degrees of freedom --- deflection and
rotation --- per node, and the pinned conditions $w=0$ at both ends. The
eigen-curvature $\kappa_{th}(x)$ and the pressure load $p(x)$ enter through
their consistent load vectors, and the membrane force is updated through the
scalar fixed point \eqref{eq:nupdate}, whose right-hand side is evaluated from
the arc length of the current state. The fixed point is contracted --- the
relaxation factor is $0.03$ at the design point --- and is iterated to a
relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, which is reached in $11$ iterations. The
implementation is verified against \eqref{eq:closed} and against the classical
limits in \S\ref{sec:verification}.

The thickness-averaged temperature $\bar\theta$ and the through-thickness
gradient that enters $\kappa_{th}$ are extracted from the conjugate solution
rather than assumed. On each of the $n_x+1=121$ columns of the wall mesh the
seven nodal and mid-side temperatures are fitted by a least-squares linear
profile in $y$; the intercept gives $\bar\theta(x)$ and the slope gives
$\gamma(x)$, hence $\kappa_{th}(x)$ through \eqref{eq:kappa}. The recovery
reproduces the imposed gradient $q''/k_s$ to $0.36\%$ in the mean over the
strip and to $2.5\%$ at the worst station, next to the pinned ends, and is
therefore consistent with \eqref{eq:kappa}; the mean recovered
eigen-curvature at the design point is $1.40015$~m$^{-1}$ against the
one-dimensional $1.40000$~m$^{-1}$, a relative difference of $10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Coupling algorithm and stopping rules}
\label{sec:coupling}

The three sub-problems are coupled by a partitioned fixed-point iteration on
the interface deflection. Because the converged state is steady, no mesh
velocity enters the fluid equations and the discrete geometric-conservation
constraint that would be mandatory in an ALE formulation
\citep{lesoinne1996geometric} does not arise; the only geometric requirement
is that each iterate use a valid, positively oriented mesh, which
\eqref{eq:blend} guarantees. The algorithm is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item given $w^k(x)$, build the body-fitted fluid mesh from \eqref{eq:blend}
and the conforming wall mesh on the reference configuration;
\item solve the flow in the deformed passage \eqref{eq:nsdim} and extract the
interface pressure;
\item solve the monolithic conjugate thermal problem
\eqref{eq:thermalweak}--\eqref{eq:monolithic} and recover $\bar\theta(x)$ and
$\kappa_{th}(x)$ column by column;
\item solve the beam--column problem \eqref{eq:beam}--\eqref{eq:nupdate} with
the pressure as transverse load and $\kappa_{th}$ as eigen-curvature, giving
$\tilde w^k$;
\item under-relax, $w^{k+1}=(1-\omega)w^k+\omega\tilde w^k$ with $\omega=0.7$,
and repeat until $\|w^{k+1}-w^k\|_\infty/\|w^{k+1}\|_\infty<10^{-4}$.
\end{enumerate}

The under-relaxation is a standard partitioned-coupling device
\citep{felippa2001partitioned}; the contraction factor observed at the design
point is $0.31$ per iteration (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(a)), so the
tolerance is met in nine iterations. The iteration stops with a documented
reason: convergence, exhaustion of the iteration budget (25), or --- more
importantly --- crossing the kinematic guard $|w|/H_0>0.45$ of bound B3.
Runs that stop for the last reason are reported as first-iteration predictors
and are explicitly marked as outside the validity of the model; they are not
presented as coupled solutions. Every stage of the program writes a
machine-readable record of the parameters, the convergence history and the
termination reason, so that the provenance of each number in
\S\ref{sec:results} is traceable to a file.

were halved and doubled together ($r=2$), so that every level is a uniform
refinement of the previous one. Table~\ref{tab:mesh} lists the levels and the
five quantities of interest: the maximum bow, the pressure drop, the mean wall
temperature rise, the peak bending stress and the mean Nusselt number.

The observed orders are $p=0.58$ for the bow, $0.69$ for the pressure drop, $0.59$
for the mean wall temperature, $0.55$ for the peak bending stress and $2.85$
for the mean Nusselt number. The corresponding indices on the production mesh
--- which is the finest of the three, and the mesh on which every number in
\S\ref{sec:results} is computed --- are $0.83\%$, $0.90\%$, $1.98\%$, $3.01\%$
and $0.23\%$.

The observed order of the deflection and of the stress is well below the
nominal second order of the discretisation, and the explanation is physical
rather than numerical. The imposed flux is discontinuous at $x=0$ and
$x=L$, where the heated strip meets the insulated casing; the wall
temperature has a logarithmic-type singularity at those two corners, and the
recovered eigen-curvature inherits it. A singularity of this type limits the
global order of any quantity obtained by differentiating the temperature
field, and it is the reason the deflection (a double integral of the
curvature) converges at $p\approx0.6$ rather than at $p=2$. Two remarks
follow. The Nusselt number, which is a ratio of integrals at a station rather
than a derivative of the temperature, converges at better than second order;
and the GCI values above are computed with the observed order rather than with
the nominal one, which is the conservative choice recommended by
\citet{roache1994perspective}.

The practical consequence is the uncertainty statement used throughout
\S\ref{sec:results}: displacements and pressure drops are reported to three
significant figures with a discretisation uncertainty below $1\%$,
temperatures to three significant figures with about $2\%$, and stresses to
three significant figures with about $3\%$. These are discretisation
uncertainties only; they do not include the modelling uncertainties of
\S\ref{sec:limitations}, which are larger and are stated separately.

\subsubsection{Code verification against analytical solutions}

The implementation is verified against five analytical objects, each

\subsubsection{Conservation checks}
\label{sec:conservation}

Three integral checks are applied to every coupled run; they are not
post-processing niceties but the controls that identify a broken solve.

\emph{Mass.} The inlet and outlet fluxes agree to one part in $10^{8}$
($2.5000000\times10^{-5}$ and $2.4999999\times10^{-5}$~m$^2$/s), and the flux
through the walls and the interface is identically zero because the no-slip
data on those boundaries are exact. This is the check that the pressure datum
is handled correctly; when the datum was imposed by replacing a continuity
equation, the same mesh produced a $0.9\%$ outflow deficit, which the present
formulation eliminates.

\emph{Energy.} The imposed flux integrated over the outer face is
$Q'=q''L=150$~W/m, and the enthalpy flux through the outlet plane relative to
the inlet temperature is $150.01$~W/m, a relative imbalance of
$8.4\times10^{-5}$. This is the combined effect of the SUPG term and of the
linear-solve tolerance and is a factor $10^{4}$ below the accuracy of the
material data.

\emph{Interface flux.} Evaluating the normal heat flux at the interface
independently from the fluid side and from the wall side gives a relative
difference of $2.7\%$ in the $L_2$ norm over the interface edges. This is a
discretisation artefact and not a modelling error: the two sides approximate
the same continuous flux with different element geometries and different
gradients. It is reported rather than suppressed because it is the honest
measure of how well the conjugate condition \eqref{eq:ifthermal} is satisfied
pointwise, and it bounds the accuracy with which \emph{local} heat-transfer
coefficients at the interface should be interpreted. The integrated flux, by
contrast, is exact by construction of the monolithic assembly.

\emph{Reproducibility.} The program has no stochastic component; two identical
runs produce bit-identical interface deflections, which is asserted by the
test suite. All results of \S\ref{sec:results} are regenerated by a single
command from the parameter file, and the figures and tables of this paper are
produced from the saved numerical output rather than transcribed
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Numerical results and discussion}
\label{sec:results}

The coupled solution is reported in the order in which the loop closes. The
closed-form baseline comes first (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), then the flow in
the deformed passage (Section~\ref{sec:flowresults}), the conjugate temperature
field (Section~\ref{sec:thermalresults}) and the wall response that produces
them (Section~\ref{sec:wall}). Sections~\ref{sec:convresults}--\ref{sec:modform}
quantify the convergence of the coupling, the sensitivity to the imposed flux,
the wall thickness, the Reynolds number and two material properties, and the
model-form error of the frozen-force closure.

Every number in this section is computed at the design point of
Table~\ref{tab:params} on the production mesh of Table~\ref{tab:mesh}, unless
a sweep is named explicitly. Sweep points are complete coupled computations,
recomputed from scratch with a new mesh, flow, thermal field and coupling
loop; they are not perturbations of the baseline.

\subsection{Closed-form baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} collects the closed-form quantities of the
formulation, distinguishing algebraic definitions from the one-dimensional
estimate of the wall temperature. The design point is hydraulically gentle:
$f_D=1.92$, a pressure drop over the heated length of $72$~Pa, a wall shear
stress of $0.6$~Pa and a mean strip pressure of $84$~Pa for the outlet datum
of Table~\ref{tab:bcs}. It is thermally aggressive: the through-thickness
temperature difference is $60$~K, the free eigen-curvature is
$1.4$~m$^{-1}$, and the blocked thermal resultant is
$2.90\times10^{4}$~N/m, that is $0.275$ of the Euler load. The linear bow is
$0.315\,H_0$, and the beam--column amplification raises it to $0.438\,H_0$
when the blocked force is held frozen. The buckling margin is $M_b=3.64$ on
the estimated wall temperature, so the strip is well below thermal buckling,
but the geometric amplification is already worth $39\%$ of the linear bow.

\subsection{The semi-analytical model against the computation}
\label{sec:asymptotics}

Table~\ref{tab:asymptotics} collects the comparison. The hydraulic result is the sharpest:
once the \emph{computed} interface shape is inserted into the lubrication integral
\eqref{eq:lubmom}, the closed form reproduces the finite-element pressure drop of
$314.508$~Pa to $+0.03\%$, so that at $\ReDh=50$ the pressure drop of the deformed passage is
a property of the shape alone and carries no discretisation or inertia error of its own. The
same integral evaluated with the kinematic idealisation $w=w_{\max}\sin^2(\pi x/L)$
underpredicts it by $15.08\%$, and Figure~\ref{fig:pressurelaw} shows how that error grows
with $a$: $-1.7\%$ at $a=0.067$, $-6.7\%$ at $a=0.228$ and $-15.1\%$ at $a=0.441$. The
sin$^2$ idealisation is thus a lower bound whose error is second order in the bow, which is
the quantitative reason why design estimates based on a nominal deflected shape deteriorate
exactly where the deformation matters.

The thermal comparison is less sharp and more instructive. The series \eqref{eq:Nuseries}
describes a straight passage; in the bowed one the local Nusselt number exceeds it
everywhere beyond the first millimetre, by $5\%$ at $x=2$~mm, by $47\%$ at $x=10$~mm and by
$68\%$ at the throat, reaching a maximum of $70\%$ just upstream of it
(Figure~\ref{fig:asymptotics}a), while in the singular entry region the two coincide. The
ratio correlates with the local stretch as
\begin{equation}
\frac{Nu(x)}{Nu_{\rm Graetz}(x)}\simeq\Bigl(\frac{H_0}{H(x)}\Bigr)^{p},\qquad
p=0.79\ \ (R^2=0.81,\ x>1\ \mathrm{mm}),
\label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
with the throat value $1.68$ against $H_0/H=1.79$ predicted for unit exponent. Two limiting
estimates bracket the exponent: a boundary layer thinned by the local acceleration
$u\propto1/H$ gives $h\propto u^{1/2}$ and $p=\tfrac12$, whereas one thinned by the wall
shear $\tau_w=6\mu Q/H^2$ gives $h\propto\tau_w^{1/2}$ and $p=1$. The measured $p=0.79$ lies
between them, as it must for a layer that is neither fully developed nor freshly started, and
\eqref{eq:scaling} is the quantitative statement of the feedback that motivates the whole
problem: the deformation raises the heat-transfer coefficient precisely where it constricts
the passage, so that the wall runs cooler than a straight-channel estimate predicts.

The wall temperature itself is $6.3\%$ higher in the computation than in the series-plus-
conduction model ($34.26$ against $32.10$~K). The gap is not a defect of the series: it is
the two-dimensional conduction in the strip, which the semi-analytical model replaces by a
linear through-thickness profile and a mean value, and which the computation resolves on the
wall mesh. It is reported here rather than absorbed into a correction factor because it is the
one quantity of the comparison that the reduced model cannot improve without abandoning its
one-dimensional structure.

\subsection{Flow in the deformed passage}
\label{sec:flowresults}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the computed velocity profiles at five streamwise
stations and the streamwise pressure distribution. The contraction produced by
the bow accelerates the core from $0.075$~m/s in the undeformed entry to
$0.134$~m/s at the throat, an increase of $79\%$ that follows the reduction of
the local height from $H_0$ to $0.56\,H_0$ almost exactly as the continuity of
the mass flux requires. The profiles remain smooth and single-peaked, the
streamwise velocity is non-negative at every degree of freedom, and no
separation bubble appears at the throat. This is the a-posteriori
justification for the do-nothing outflow condition and for the attached,
laminar assumption of A6.

The hydraulic consequence is large. The pressure drop over the whole passage
rises from $144$~Pa for the undeformed channel --- the exact slit value
$12\mu U L_{\mathrm{tot}}/H_0^2$ --- to $314.5$~Pa, that is by a factor
$2.19$. The increase is not a small perturbation of the rigid-channel result
and would be missed entirely by a lubrication estimate based on the undeformed
geometry. The mean wall shear on the deformed interface is $1.30$~Pa against
$0.59$~Pa for the undeformed channel, a factor $2.2$ that matches the
pressure-drop ratio because the flow remains in the same viscous regime.

\subsection{Conjugate heat transfer}
\label{sec:thermalresults}

Figure~\ref{fig:thermal} reports the local Nusselt number
$Nu=2H_0h/k_f$ along the heated segment and the temperature levels of the
coupled solution. Three features are worth recording.

First, the local Nusselt number rises to $14.5$ at the very first station,
where the wall-to-bulk temperature difference vanishes and the ratio is not
resolvable; away from that singularity it falls to $8.2$ at
$x\approx5$~mm, rises to a second maximum of $9.5$ near mid-strip and then
decreases to $6.2$ at the trailing edge as the passage widens again and the
boundary layer thickens. This is \emph{not} a fully developed channel value:
the thermal entry length of the present passage is comparable with the strip
length, so no single Nusselt number characterises the wall. The mean over the
segment, $\overline{Nu}=8.69$, exceeds every fully developed value precisely
because the entry region is weighted in it, and it should not be quoted as a
fully developed coefficient.

Second, the thickness-averaged wall temperature rises by $34.3$~K on average
and by at most $35.4$~K, and the outer face, which carries the imposed flux,
reaches $65.9$~K above the inlet. The one-dimensional estimate of $32.3$~K in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} therefore lies $6.2\%$ below the computed value, and
the difference is the convective resistance of the developing boundary layer,
which the estimate replaces by the fully developed $H1$ coefficient. This is a
mild, unremarkable difference that would not affect a design decision, but it
is the reason the coupling is iterated rather than evaluated once.

Third, the through-thickness gradient recovered from the two-dimensional wall
solution reproduces $q''/k_s$ to $0.36\%$ in the mean over the strip and to
$2.5\%$ at the worst station, which sits next to the pinned ends: the wall is
thin enough that its conduction problem is essentially one-dimensional in the
core of the strip, and the end deviation is the trace of the axial conduction
admitted near the casing. The resulting eigen-curvature has a mean of
$1.40015$~m$^{-1}$ against the one-dimensional $1.40000$~m$^{-1}$, a relative
difference of $10^{-4}$, which is the quantitative justification for the
recovery operator used in the structure sub-problem.

\subsection{Wall response and the coupled state}
\label{sec:wallresponse}

Table~\ref{tab:coupled} summarises the coupled solution and
Figure~\ref{fig:wall} shows the deflected shape and the bending moment. The
strip bows to $w_{\max}=2.207\times10^{-4}$~m, that is $0.441\,H_0$, with the

\subsection{Convergence of the coupling}
\label{sec:convresults}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(a) shows the history of the partitioned iteration
at the design point. The relative change of the interface deflection decreases
by a factor of about three per iteration once the first two iterations have
established the thermal field, and the tolerance of $10^{-4}$ is met at
iteration nine. The final state satisfies the same tolerance in the energy
balance ($8.4\times10^{-5}$) and in the mass balance

\subsection{Response to the imposed flux and to the wall thickness}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} and Table~\ref{tab:sweep} report the coupled
response to the imposed flux, recomputed from scratch at each point. The bow
grows slightly faster than linearly in $q''$: $0.067\,H_0$ at
$1$~kW/m$^2$, $0.142\,H_0$ at $2$, $0.228\,H_0$ at $3$, $0.326\,H_0$ at $4$ and
$0.441\,H_0$ at $5$~kW/m$^2$. The excess over linearity is the geometric
amplification of \eqref{eq:nupdate}, and it is modest because the force relief
grows with the bow. The pressure drop grows more strongly, from $155$~Pa to
$314.5$~Pa, because the passage height enters it with a high inverse power:
even at $1$~kW/m$^2$, where the bow is only $0.067\,H_0$, the pressure drop is
already $7.4\%$ above the undeformed value. The mean wall temperature grows
almost linearly, from $6.5$~K to $34.3$~K, with a slope of
$6.9$~K per kW/m$^2$ against the $6.45$ predicted by the one-dimensional
estimate; the small excess is again the developing boundary layer.

Because the coupling is stopped once the predicted bow exceeds $0.45\,H_0$,
the coupled sweep brackets rather than reaches the kinematic ceiling. The
ceiling itself is located on the reduced-model envelope, evaluated with the
same wall solver over a fine flux sweep and the one-dimensional thermal input:
$|w|/H_0=0.45$ at $5.10$~kW/m$^2$ and $|w|/H_0=0.5$ at $5.49$~kW/m$^2$. The
coupled guard is crossed between $5.00$ and $5.25$~kW/m$^2$
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}), in agreement with the envelope. On the same envelope
the strength ceiling $|\sigma|_{\max}=0.2\sigma_y$ is reached at
$5.95$~kW/m$^2$ and $0.5\sigma_y$ at $9.82$~kW/m$^2$, while the buckling
margin $M_b=2$ is reached at $9.10$~kW/m$^2$. For this geometry and material
the \emph{deformation} bound is therefore the binding design constraint, and
it precedes the strength bound by only $17\%$ in flux, which is smaller than
the uncertainty of the one-dimensional wall-temperature estimate on which the
envelope rests. The practical conclusion is that deformation and strength
become limiting almost simultaneously, and that a design based on strength
alone would have little margin against a change of passage height.

The thickness sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}(b) shows the expected
strong sensitivity: increasing $d$ from $3$ to $5$~mm reduces the bow from
$0.441$ to $0.377\,H_0$ and the peak bending stress from $4.39$ to
$3.61$~MPa, while the mean wall temperature rises from $34.3$ to $54.0$~K
because the through-thickness resistance increases. Thinner walls are outside
the validity of the model at the design flux: at $d=2.5$~mm the predicted bow
reaches $0.466\,H_0$ and the run is stopped by the guard. The trade-off
between deformation and temperature rise is therefore direct and lies entirely
inside the range where the reduced model is valid, which is the practically
useful part of this figure.

\subsection{Reynolds-number sensitivity}
\label{sec:reynolds}

Table~\ref{tab:reynolds} and Figure~\ref{fig:reynolds} report the coupled
solution for inlet velocities between $0.025$ and $0.1$~m/s at the design
flux. The bow decreases monotonically with the Reynolds number, from
$0.4459\,H_0$ at $\ReDh=25$ to $0.4386\,H_0$ at $\ReDh=100$, because a faster
flow removes the same flux at a lower wall temperature: the mean wall rise
falls from $35.1$ to $33.8$~K and the compressive resultant from $0.290$ to
$0.279$ of the Euler load. The sensitivity is weak --- a factor of four in
Reynolds number changes the bow by $1.6\%$ --- which is a direct consequence
of the thermal boundary condition: with an imposed flux, the wall temperature
is set by the through-thickness conduction and by the convective resistance in
series, and the convective resistance is the smaller of the two at this
Prandtl number. The pressure drop, by contrast, grows almost exactly linearly
with $U$ ($159$, $315$, $469$ and $624$~Pa at the four velocities) because the
flow remains in the viscous regime, and the mean Nusselt number grows from
$8.30$ to $9.51$ as the thermal entry length becomes shorter relative to the
strip.

One further point at $U=0.01$~m/s ($\ReDh=10$) is not listed in
Table~\ref{tab:reynolds}: at that velocity the predicted bow reaches
$0.455\,H_0$, the kinematic guard of bound B3 stops the iteration, and the run
is reported only as a first-iteration predictor. The table therefore contains
the four converged points and nothing else, which is the convention used for
every sweep in this paper.

\subsection{Wall conductivity and thermal expansion}
\label{sec:material}

Figure~\ref{fig:material} and Tables~\ref{tab:material}--\ref{tab:expansion}
report the two material sensitivities that matter most. The wall conductivity
controls the through-thickness gradient and the surface temperature
simultaneously, and its effect is strong: raising $k_s$ from $0.25$ to
$0.60$~W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ reduces the mean wall rise from $34.3$ to
$14.8$~K, the eigen-curvature by the same factor ($58\%$), the bow from
$0.441$ to $0.150\,H_0$, the peak bending stress from $4.39$ to $0.66$~MPa and
the pressure drop from $314.5$ to $172.0$~Pa. The mean Nusselt number, by
contrast, falls only from $8.69$ to $6.87$, because it is a fluid-side
quantity referenced to the undeformed height: most of the change in the
hydraulic resistance is carried by the reduced bow rather than by the heat
transfer coefficient itself. The conductivity therefore acts as the strongest
single design lever on the deformation, which is consistent with the
$1/k_s$ scaling of the eigen-curvature in \eqref{eq:kappa} and with the
central role of wall conduction in conjugate channel problems
\citep{hassab2013effect,quintero2017laminar,rahimi2024numerical}.

The thermal expansion coefficient is the other multiplicative factor in
\eqref{eq:kappa}, and its effect is correspondingly linear in the small-bow
regime and superlinear above it: reducing $\alpha$ from $5.0$ to
$1.5\times10^{-5}$~K$^{-1}$ reduces the bow from $0.441$ to $0.103\,H_0$
(a factor $4.3$ against the factor $3.3$ in $\alpha$) and the peak bending
stress from $4.39$ to $0.29$~MPa, while the mean wall temperature falls by
only $1.6$~K because the thermal problem is almost decoupled from the
mechanical one. The superlinearity is the geometric amplification again: at
small $\alpha$ the membrane force is far from the Euler load and the response
is nearly linear, whereas at the design point $|N|/N_{cr}=0.283$ and the
amplification of the linear bow is $40\%$.

\subsection{Model-form error of the frozen-force closure}
\label{sec:modform}

The frozen-force closure --- evaluating the membrane force from the blocked
state and holding it constant, as is customary in design estimates --- is an
approximation to the axial compatibility \eqref{eq:nupdate}.
Figure~\ref{fig:modelform} quantifies its cost over the whole admissible
range, using the same wall solver and the same one-dimensional thermal input
for both closures, so that the comparison isolates the closure and nothing
else. The bow is over-predicted by $0.03\%$ at $1.1$~kW/m$^2$, by $0.16\%$ at
$2.4$~kW/m$^2$, by $1.2\%$ at $5.1$~kW/m$^2$, by $4.3\%$ at
$7.4$~kW/m$^2$ and by $14.9\%$ at $10$~kW/m$^2$; the bending moment is
over-predicted by more than the bow ($0.43\%$, $1.2\%$, $4.4\%$, $11.1\%$ and
$30.7\%$ at the same loads) because the moment is a second derivative of a
shape whose curvature error grows faster than its amplitude error. The force
relief, $(N_\theta-N)/N_\theta$, reaches $12\%$ at $10$~kW/m$^2$.

The conclusion is quantitative and useful. The frozen-force closure is
adequate below about $2.5$~kW/m$^2$, where the bow error is below $0.2\%$ and
the moment error below $1.3\%$; it is marginal between $2.5$ and
$5$~kW/m$^2$, where the errors become comparable with the discretisation
uncertainty of \S\ref{sec:grid}; and it becomes unsafe for stress work above
$5$~kW/m$^2$, where the moment error is already $4.4\%$ and rises to $11.1\%$
at $7.4$~kW/m$^2$ while the model itself is still within its kinematic bounds.
Interpolating between the computed points, the closure error at the kinematic
ceiling of $5.5$~kW/m$^2$ is about $1.8\%$ for the bow and $5.7\%$ for the
moment: tolerable for a preliminary estimate of the passage height, not for a
stress calculation. That is the recommendation which follows from this
figure.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the loop matters}
\label{sec:whyloop}

The results above describe a configuration in which three effects that are
usually treated separately act at once: conjugate conduction through a wall of
finite thickness, thermoelastic bending of an axially restrained strip, and
viscous flow in a passage whose height the strip controls. Each of the three
is well understood in isolation. Conjugate wall conduction is quantified in
the studies of \citet{hassab2013effect} and \citet{quintero2017laminar};
thermoelastic buckling of heated plates and shells is classical
\citep{mansfield1962bending,hoff1964buckling,ross1965buckling} and remains an
active area \citep{mehrabi2025axisymmetric,sadeghi2022generalized}; and
compliant-channel hydraulics is captured by the flow-rate relations of
\citet{christov2018flow}, \citet{wang2019theory} and \citet{boyko2022flow}.
What the present computation adds is the closure of the loop: the wall
temperature that drives the deformation is the temperature that the deformed
passage produces, and the passage height that controls the convective
resistance is the height that the wall temperature sets.

The quantitative signature of the closure is the disparity of the two loads.
At the design point the thermal resultant exceeds the Euler load fraction
$0.283$, while the pressure produces a bow three orders of magnitude smaller
than the thermal one. An analysis that treated the pressure as the load would
therefore predict a deformation of $10^{-7}$~m and a pressure drop within
$0.1\%$ of the rigid value, whereas the coupled answer differs from the rigid
value by $119\%$. The mechanism is thermal, the consequence is hydraulic, and
neither can be inferred from the other without solving both.

\subsection{Thermal--hydraulic performance}
\label{sec:performance}

The constriction improves the heat-transfer coefficient per unit wall area
while it costs pumping power, and the two effects are not of equal size. Using
the mean Nusselt number referenced to the undeformed height as a proxy for the
coefficient and the pressure drop as the cost, the ratio $\overline{Nu}/\Delta p$
falls from $4.23\times10^{-2}$~Pa$^{-1}$ at $1$~kW/m$^2$ to
$2.76\times10^{-2}$~Pa$^{-1}$ at $5$~kW/m$^2$, a degradation of $35\%$ as the
bow grows from $0.067$ to $0.441\,H_0$. The bare $\overline{Nu}$ rises by
$33\%$ over the same range, so a performance comparison based on the Nusselt
number alone would reverse the conclusion. This is the same lesson that the
flexible-fin and flexible-wall studies report from the heat-transfer side
\citep{soti2015flow,derakhshandeh2026fluid,chowdhury2025fluid}, and it is the
reason the coupled computation rather than a heat-transfer correlation is the
appropriate design tool for this class of passages.

\subsection{Where the model stops}
\label{sec:wherestop}

The model has a genuine ceiling and it is reached within the range of fluxes
that the configuration would be used at. For the present geometry and
material, the kinematic guard is crossed at $5.10$~kW/m$^2$
($0.45\,H_0$), the strength ceiling $0.2\sigma_y$ at $5.95$~kW/m$^2$, and the
buckling margin $M_b=2$ at $9.10$~kW/m$^2$. The ordering --- deformation,
strength, stability --- is a consequence of the scaling of
\eqref{eq:critical}: the critical temperature scales as $(d/L)^2$, whereas
both the bow and the bending stress scale with the eigen-curvature
$(1+\nu)\alpha q''/k_s$ and hence linearly in the flux. A thinner or longer
strip moves the deformation ceiling down faster than the stability ceiling,
which is the opposite of what a stability-first design heuristic would
suggest. The other side of the same coin is that a stiffer, more conductive
wall raises the deformation ceiling without changing the stability margin at
all, which is why $k_s$ and $E$ are the design levers of choice
(\S\ref{sec:material}).

\subsection{Uncertainty budget}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

The uncertainty of the reported numbers has four contributions, of which only
the first is quantified here.
(i) Discretisation: the GCI values of \S\ref{sec:grid}, namely $0.8\%$ for the
bow, $0.9\%$ for the pressure drop, $2.0\%$ for the mean wall temperature and
$3.0\%$ for the peak bending stress.
(ii) Material data: the wall properties are representative datasheet values,
and the sensitivities of \S\ref{sec:material} translate a $\pm10\%$ change of
$k_s$ into roughly $\mp12\%$ of the bow and a $\pm10\%$ change of $\alpha$ into
roughly $\pm10\%$ of the bow at the design point.
(iii) Fluid properties: constant-property water over a computed passage
temperature rise of at most $2.8$~K, with a viscosity sensitivity of about
$7\%$ over that range.
(iv) Radiation from the outer face: at the computed face temperature of
$359$~K with an emissivity of $0.9$ and surroundings at ambient, the net
radiative flux is about $470$~W/m$^2$, that is $9\%$ of the imposed
$5$~kW/m$^2$. Radiation is not modelled, so the computed bow is an upper
bound by roughly the corresponding fraction. This is the largest unmodelled
effect in the budget and it is listed again in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The following restrictions are intrinsic to the results presented and should
be read as part of them. None is repaired by a change of discretisation.

\emph{Reduced structural model.} The wall is solved as a beam--column strip,
not as a two-dimensional thermoelastic continuum. The reduction is justified
at $L/d=10$ and small slopes, and its axial-compatibility error is quantified
in \S\ref{sec:modform}; the through-thickness distribution of the mechanical
stress is, however, not resolved, and the reported bending stress is the
extreme-fibre value $6|M|/d^2$ rather than a computed two-dimensional field.
A full plane-strain finite-element solution of the strip, coupled to the same
fluid and thermal solvers, is the natural next step and is not attempted here.

\emph{Steady, two-dimensional, laminar, constant-property flow.} Unsteady
effects are excluded, although the time scales suggest that they would matter
for a transient start-up: the wall conduction time is $51.5$~s against a fluid
residence time of $0.6$~s, so the coupled system would pass through a sequence
of quasi-steady states with an almost isothermal wall before the thermal bow
develops. Three-dimensional and transitional effects are outside the stated
Reynolds-number range of B1. Property variation is bounded by the band of A2
and is not modelled; the computed wall temperatures of $35$~K above ambient
are large enough that the temperature dependence of the polymer properties
would matter for a quantitative thermal-stress prediction.

\emph{Radiation.} The outer face radiates; the estimated flux is about $9\%$
of the imposed flux at the design point (\S\ref{sec:uncertainty}). The
computed deformation is therefore an upper bound, and a cooled or reflective
casing would change it measurably.

\emph{Idealised supports.} The pins are imposed on the full end edge rather
than at the corners, and the casing is rigid and adiabatic. Both are
idealisations whose error scales with the end-layer width $O(d)$ and is
therefore a few per cent of the global stiffness at $L/d=10$. They also
suppress the axial wall conduction that a real casing would admit, which the
conjugate studies of \citet{hassab2013effect} and \citet{arici2010heat} show
to be non-negligible near the ends of a heated segment.

\emph{Neglected physics.} Contact between the deformed strip and the opposite
wall at large bow is not modelled; it is what the kinematic guard of B3
conservatively anticipates, but no contact condition is imposed. Contact
resistance at the interface is excluded by A8, and any fouling or coating
would break that assumption.

\emph{Material and experimental basis.} The wall properties are representative
datasheet values for a PEEK-class polymer, not a certified grade, and no
experiment is reported. The computed stresses of $4.4$~MPa (bending) and
$10.3$~MPa (membrane) are far below the yield strength of the class, but the
viscoelastic and ageing behaviour of the polymer, which the elastic model does
not represent, would matter for a long-service application. The results should
be read as a verified computation of a stated model rather than as a
validation of that model against hardware; that comparison is the principal
open item, and the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params} was chosen to be
buildable for exactly that purpose.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A heated channel wall that is thin, axially restrained and thermally loaded
deforms by an amount that is large compared with the hydraulic forcing, and
that deformation changes the passage it bounds. For the configuration studied
here --- a plane channel of $0.5$~mm undeformed height with a $3$~mm strip
over $30$~mm, water at $\ReDh=50$ and $q''=5$~kW/m$^2$ --- the coupled,
verified computation gives the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item It is shown that the strip bows $2.21\times10^{-4}$~m, or $0.441$ of the undeformed
passage height. The bow is driven by the thermal eigen-curvature and amplified
by $40\%$ over the linear bow by the compressive membrane force, which reaches
$0.283$ of the Euler load. Pressure contributes $0.11\%$ of the deflection,
three orders of magnitude less.
\item It is shown that the bow more than doubles the hydraulic resistance, from $144$ to
$314.5$~Pa, and accelerates the core flow by $79\%$ while raising the mean
wall shear from $0.59$ to $1.30$~Pa. A pressure-only estimate of the same
configuration would miss this entirely, because the pressure-driven bow is
$9.2\times10^{-8}$~m.
\item It is shown that the conjugate wall temperature rise is $34.3$~K, $6.2\%$ above the
one-dimensional estimate, because the developing boundary layer raises the
convective resistance over the first part of the strip. The local Nusselt
number varies from $8.2$ just downstream of the leading edge to a second
maximum of $9.5$ at mid-strip and $6.2$ at the trailing edge, so no single
value characterises the strip and the mean of $8.69$ should not be quoted as a
fully developed coefficient.
\item It is shown that the frozen-force closure of the wall equation is adequate below about
$2.5$~kW/m$^2$ and over-predicts the bow by $14.9\%$ and the moment by
$30.7\%$ at $10$~kW/m$^2$. The axial-compatibility update of
\eqref{eq:nupdate} is therefore required for quantitative work above about
$2.5$~kW/m$^2$, and for any stress calculation at the operating ceiling.
\item It is shown that the binding constraint on the operating range is the kinematic ceiling
of the reduced model, crossed at $5.10$~kW/m$^2$ ($0.45\,H_0$), before the
strength ceiling of $0.2\sigma_y$ at $5.95$~kW/m$^2$ and well before the
buckling margin $M_b=2$ at $9.10$~kW/m$^2$. The ordering follows from the
$(d/L)^2$ scaling of the critical temperature against the linear scaling of
the eigen-curvature with flux.
\item It is shown that thermal--hydraulic performance, measured as $\overline{Nu}/\Delta p$,
degrades by $35\%$ as the flux rises from $1$ to $5$~kW/m$^2$, even though the
Nusselt number itself rises by $33\%$. Performance claims based on the
Nusselt number alone are therefore unsafe for thermally deforming passages.
\item It is shown that the two equations of the strip model are the Euler--Lagrange
equations of a single two-field functional, so that the axial-compatibility condition
\eqref{eq:nupdate} is a consequence of the kinematic constraint of the pinned ends rather than
an additional assumption, and that the three terms discarded in the reduction are bounded by
$4.0\%$ (transverse shear), $1.4\times10^{-4}$ (finite strain) and the stress-recovery error
of \S\ref{sec:modform}.
\item It is shown that the axial-compatibility map is a contraction whenever
$N_\theta<N_{cr}/3$, with the explicit rate $q=2N_\theta/(N_{cr}-N_\theta)=0.757$ at the
design point, so that the coupled problem has a unique solution and the fixed-point iteration
converges geometrically; the measured derivative of the map is $1.6\times10^{-5}$.
\item It is shown that the hydraulic resistance of the bowed passage is a Gauss hypergeometric
function of the normalised bow, $\Delta p/\Delta p_0={}_2F_1(3,\tfrac12;1;a)$, that it
reproduces the coupled computation to $0.03\%$ when the computed shape is inserted, and that
the kinematic $\sin^2$ idealisation is a lower bound whose error grows to $15\%$ at
$a=0.44$.
\item It is shown that the thermal-entry problem in the straight passage has the exact
Sturm--Liouville solution \eqref{eq:graetz} with $\lambda_1=3.696$ and $Nu_\infty=70/13$,
that the deformation raises the local Nusselt number above that reference by up to $70\%$,
and that the excess collapses onto $\bigl(H_0/H\bigr)^{0.79}$ with $R^2=0.81$, between the
two exponents $(\tfrac12,1)$ that bracket a layer thinned by acceleration and by wall shear.
\end{enumerate}

The computational formulation that produces these numbers is verified against
the classical plane-channel solutions to better than $0.5\%$, reproduces its
closed-form beam--column solution to $2.5\times10^{-8}$, conserves mass to one
part in $10^{8}$ and energy to $8\times10^{-5}$, and converges in nine
coupling iterations with a measured contraction factor of $0.31$. Its
discretisation uncertainties are below $1\%$ for displacements, $2\%$ for
temperatures and $3\%$ for stresses. Its limitations are those of
\S\ref{sec:limitations}; the most consequential is the reduced structural
model, whose replacement by a two-dimensional thermoelastic continuum coupled
to the same fluid and thermal solvers is the obvious next step, and the second
most consequential is the omission of radiation, which bounds the present
deformation predictions from above.

\section{Closed-form solution of the beam--column equation}
\label{app:closedform}

This appendix derives \eqref{eq:closed} and the pressure-only limit used in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline}, and records the contraction property of the axial
fixed point.

\subsection*{A.1 Homogeneous solution with imposed end curvature}

With a constant eigen-curvature, $k\equiv1$, and no transverse load,
$\Pi=0$, equation \eqref{eq:beamdim} is
\begin{equation}
W''''+\lambda^2W''=0,
\qquad W(0)=W(1)=0,\qquad W''(0)=W''(1)=-\beta .
\label{eq:appode}
\end{equation}
The general solution is
\begin{equation}
W=A+B\xi+C\cos\lambda\xi+D\sin\lambda\xi .
\label{eq:appgeneral}
\end{equation}
The four conditions give, in the order in which they are applied,
\begin{align}
W(0)=0 &\;\Longrightarrow\; A=-C, \label{eq:appc1}\\
W''(0)=-\beta &\;\Longrightarrow\; -\lambda^2C=-\beta
\;\Longrightarrow\; C=\beta/\lambda^2, \label{eq:appc2}\\
W''(1)=-\beta &\;\Longrightarrow\; -\lambda^2\bigl(C\cos\lambda+D\sin\lambda\bigr)
=-\beta \;\Longrightarrow\; D=C\,\frac{1-\cos\lambda}{\sin\lambda},
\label{eq:appc3}\\
W(1)=0 &\;\Longrightarrow\; B=-C(1-\cos\lambda)-D\sin\lambda=0 ,
\label{eq:appc4}
\end{align}
using $\cos\lambda-1=-(1-\cos\lambda)$ in the last step. Substituting
\eqref{eq:appc1}--\eqref{eq:appc4} into \eqref{eq:appgeneral} gives
equation \eqref{eq:closed} of the main text,
\begin{equation}
W(\xi)=\frac{\beta}{\lambda^2}\left[
\frac{1-\cos\lambda}{\sin\lambda}\sin\lambda\xi+\cos\lambda\xi-1\right].
\label{eq:appclosed}
\end{equation}
As $\lambda\to0$, $\cos\lambda\xi-1\simeq-\lambda^2\xi^2/2$,
$(1-\cos\lambda)/\sin\lambda\simeq\lambda/2$ and $\sin\lambda\xi\simeq
\lambda\xi$, so the bracket tends to $\lambda^2\xi(1-\xi)/2$ and
$W\to\beta\xi(1-\xi)/2$, the parabolic bow $w=\kappa_{th}x(L-x)/2$ quoted in
\S\ref{sec:closedform}. The solution \eqref{eq:appclosed} is unbounded as
$\lambda\to\pi$; that is the Euler load of \eqref{eq:critical}, at which the
linearised problem ceases to have a unique solution and the nonlinear
compatibility \eqref{eq:nupdate} is what keeps the physical problem finite.

\subsection*{A.2 Uniform pressure load}

For $\beta=0$, uniform load $\Pi$ and $\lambda\to0$, the particular integral
$W_p=\Pi\xi^2/(2\lambda^2)$ combines with the homogeneous solution in the same
way to give the classical clamped-free beam result
\begin{equation}
w(x)=\frac{f x^2(L-x)^2}{24D_s},
\qquad
w_{\max}=\frac{5fL^4}{384D_s},
\label{eq:apppressure}
\end{equation}
which is reproduced by the finite-element solver to
$2.5\times10^{-9}$ (\S\ref{sec:verification}). For $\lambda\neq0$ the
pressure term adds to \eqref{eq:appclosed} a contribution of the same
structure with $\beta$ replaced by $\Pi/\lambda^2$; at the design point its
magnitude is $10^{-3}$ of the thermal bow, which is bound B5.

\subsection*{A.3 Contraction of the axial fixed point}

The compatibility update \eqref{eq:nupdate} is the scalar map
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(N)=N_\theta-\frac{Ed}{2(1-\nu^2)}\bigl\langle w'^2(N)\bigr\rangle ,
\label{eq:appmap}
\end{equation}
in which $w(N)$ solves \eqref{eq:beam} for the current force. Since
$\langle w'^2\rangle$ increases with $N$ (a larger compressive force gives a
larger bow for the same eigen-curvature), $\mathcal{N}$ is decreasing, so the
map is a contraction whenever its slope exceeds $-1$ in magnitude. Writing
$w=H_0W(\lambda)$ and using $\dd\lambda^2/\dd N=L^2/D_s$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\mathcal{N}}{\dd N}
=-\frac{E d H_0^2}{2(1-\nu^2)L}\,\frac{\dd}{\dd\lambda^2}
\int_0^1\!\Bigl(\frac{\dd W}{\dd\xi}\Bigr)^2\dd\xi ,
\label{eq:appslope}
\end{equation}
whose magnitude grows without bound as $\lambda\to\pi$ (the bow, and with it
the arc length, responds without limit as the Euler load is approached). At
the design point $\lambda^2=\pi^2|N|/N_{cr}=0.279\pi^2$, far from the
singularity, so the map is strongly contracted; moving towards the buckling
margin it contracts more slowly, which is why the relaxation factor of $0.03$
used in the implementation is retained rather than the natural fixed-point
iteration. With that factor the fixed point reaches $10^{-9}$ in eleven
iterations at the design point.

\section{Weak forms and algorithmic details}
\label{app:weak}

\subsection*{B.1 Flow}

Find $(\bu,p)\in V\times Q$, with $V$ the space of $P_2$ vector fields
satisfying the no-slip and inlet data and $Q$ the space of continuous $P_1$
functions, such that for all $(\mathbf v,q)\in V_0\times Q$,
\begin{equation}
\int_{\Om_f}\Bigl[2\mu\D(\bu):\D(\mathbf v)
+\rho\,(\bu\cdot\nabla)\bu\cdot\mathbf v-p\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v
-q\,\nabla\cdot\bu\Bigr]\dd\Om=0,
\label{eq:weakflow}
\end{equation}
with the outflow boundary term omitted (do-nothing condition) and the inlet
profile imposed strongly. The convective term is linearised by Picard
iteration; the discrete system is a saddle-point problem whose inf--sup
stability is inherited from the Taylor--Hood pair
\citep{brezzi1991mixed,hood1974navier}.

\subsection*{B.2 Conjugate energy}

Find $T\in W$, with $W$ the space of continuous $P_2$ functions on the joint
numbering that satisfy $T=T_{\mathrm{in}}$ on the inlet, such that for all
test functions $\phi\in W_0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\int_{\Om_f}\bigl(k_f\nabla T\cdot\nabla\phi
+\rho c_p(\bu\cdot\nabla T)\phi\bigr)\dd\Om
+\int_{\Om_s^0}k_s\nabla T\cdot\nabla\phi\,\dd\Om\\
&\qquad+\int_{\Ga_h^0}q''\phi\,\dd\Ga
+\underbrace{\int_{\Ga_{\mathrm{if}}}\bigl(-k_f\nabla T_f\cdot\bn
-k_s\nabla T_s\cdot\bn\bigr)\phi\,\dd\Ga}_{=\,0\ \text{by sharing}}
+\sum_e\int_{\Om_f^e}\tau_e\rho c_p(\bu\cdot\nabla T)(\bu\cdot\nabla\phi)\dd\Om=0,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weakthermal}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_e$ from \eqref{eq:supg}. The underbraced term is the only place
where the interface enters, and it vanishes identically because the trace and
the normal are one and the same on both sides: this is the algebraic content
of the monolithic assembly \eqref{eq:monolithic}.

\subsection*{B.3 Structure}

Multiplying \eqref{eq:beam} by a test function $v$ with $v(0)=v(L)=0$,
integrating by parts twice and using the natural conditions
$D_s(w''+\kappa_{th})=0$ at both ends gives
\begin{equation}
\int_0^L D_s\bigl(w''+\kappa_{th}\bigr)v''\,\dd x
-\int_0^L N\,w'v'\,\dd x=\int_0^L f\,v\,\dd x ,
\label{eq:weakbeam}
\end{equation}
which is discretised with cubic Hermite functions $H_i^{(0)},H_i^{(1)}$ on
the uniform grid, two degrees of freedom per node, and integrated with
three-point Gauss quadrature. The bilinear form is symmetric for fixed $N$,
so the sub-problem is a symmetric linear solve followed by the scalar update
\eqref{eq:nupdate}; the numerical values of the element matrices are checked
against \eqref{eq:appclosed} and \eqref{eq:apppressure} by the test suite.

\section{Proofs of the theorems of Section~\ref{sec:analytical}}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection*{D.1 Theorem~\ref{thm:contraction}: contraction of the axial map}

Let $w_N$ solve \eqref{eq:beam} with a constant membrane force $N$ and the boundary data of
\eqref{eq:beam}. Write the problem as $\mathcal L_Nw_N=g$ with
$\mathcal L_N=D_s\partial_x^4+N\partial_x^2$ and $g=f-D_s\kappa_{th}''$, and let
$(\cdot,\cdot)$ denote the $L^2(0,L)$ inner product. Differentiating in $N$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_N\,\partial_Nw_N=-\partial_x^2w_N ,
\label{eq:D1}
\end{equation}
with homogeneous boundary data. Multiplying \eqref{eq:D1} by $w_N$, integrating by parts and
using the self-adjointness of $\mathcal L_N$ on the admissible space gives
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\partial_Nw_N,\mathcal L_Nw_N\bigr)
=\bigl(w_N',\partial_Nw_N'\bigr)-\bigl(w_N'',D_s\,\partial_Nw_N''\bigr)
=-\bigl(w_N'',w_N\bigr),
\label{eq:D2}
\end{equation}
so that, after another integration by parts and use of the coercivity of $\mathcal L_N$,
whose lowest eigenvalue is $D_s\pi^4(1-N/N_{cr})/L^4$,
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\partial_Nw_N'\bigr\|^2\ \le\ \frac{\|w_N'\|^2}{N_{cr}-N}.
\label{eq:D3}
\end{equation}
Now $c\langle w_N'^2\rangle=N_\theta-N$ by \eqref{eq:map}, hence
\begin{equation}
\bigl|\mathcal F'(N)\bigr|
=2c\,\bigl|\langle w_N',\partial_Nw_N'\rangle\bigr|
\le2c\,\|w_N'\|\,\|\partial_Nw_N'\|
\le\frac{2\,(N_\theta-N)}{N_{cr}-N}
\le\frac{2N_\theta}{N_{cr}-N_\theta}=q .
\label{eq:D4}
\end{equation}
For $N_\theta<N_{cr}/3$ one has $q<1$; moreover $\mathcal F$ is continuous and
$0\le\mathcal F(N)\le N_\theta$, so $\mathcal F$ maps $[0,N_\theta]$ into itself. The Banach
fixed-point theorem then gives a unique $N^*$ and the geometric estimate \eqref{eq:rate}. The
same argument applied to the coupled map, with the thermal sub-problem replacing the fixed
$\bar\theta$, gives the contraction of the outer loop quoted in \S\ref{sec:coupling}.

\subsection*{D.2 Theorem~\ref{thm:pressure}: the hypergeometric resistance}

With $H=H_0(1-a\sin^2s)$, $s=\pi x/L$,
\begin{equation}
\int_0^L\frac{\dd x}{H^3}=\frac{L}{\pi H_0^3}\int_0^\pi
\frac{\dd s}{(1-a\sin^2s)^3}.
\label{eq:D5}
\end{equation}
Because the integrand is symmetric about $\pi/2$ and $\pi$, the integral over $(0,\pi)$ is
twice that over $(0,\pi/2)$, and the substitution $t=\sin^2s$ gives the Euler integral
\begin{equation}
\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\dd s}{(1-a\sin^2s)^3}
=\frac{\pi}{2}\,{}_2F_1\!\Bigl(3,\tfrac12;1;a\Bigr),
\label{eq:D6}
\end{equation}
which is the standard beta-integral representation
$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2\alpha-1}s\cos^{2\beta-1}s\,(1-a\sin^2s)^{-\gamma}\dd s
=\tfrac12B(\alpha,\beta)\,{}_2F_1(\gamma,\alpha;\alpha+\beta;a)$ with $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac12$
and $\gamma=3$. Combining \eqref{eq:D5} and \eqref{eq:D6} with $\Delta p=12\mu Q\int H^{-3}$
gives \eqref{eq:dpclosed}. The series in \eqref{eq:dpclosed} follows from
$(3)_n=n!(n+2)!/2$ and $(1/2)_n/(n!)=(2n-1)!!/(2^n n!)$.

A bound on the truncation of the shape is useful in design. If $w$ differs from the kinematic
shape by $\delta w$ with $\|\delta w\|_\infty\le\eta H_0$, then
$\delta(\Delta p)/\Delta p\le3\eta/(1-a)^{4}$, so that a $5\%$ error in the assumed shape at
$a=0.44$ produces at most a $24\%$ error in the resistance --- which is the order of the
$15\%$ observed in Figure~\ref{fig:pressurelaw}b, and the reason the computed shape must be
used in \eqref{eq:dpclosed} whenever it is available.

\subsection*{D.3 Theorem~\ref{thm:graetz}: completeness and coefficients}

The operator $\mathcal S\psi=-\psi''$ with Neumann data on $(0,1)$ and weight $w(Y)=6Y(1-Y)$
is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite in $L^2_w$; its spectrum is discrete,
$0=\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\dots$, and the eigenfunctions form a complete orthogonal set.
The zero eigenvalue carries the constant eigenfunction, and therefore every $\psi_n$ with
$n\ge1$ has zero weighted mean, $\langle\psi_n\rangle_w=0$. Expanding the inlet condition
$\theta(0,Y)=0$ in that basis gives $A_n=-\langle f,\psi_n\rangle_w/\langle\psi_n,\psi_n\rangle_w$;
because the perturbation carries no wall flux, the wall temperature in
\eqref{eq:Nuseries} is $\Delta T^*[f(1)+\sum A_n\psi_n(1)e^{-\lambda_n^2X}]$, and division by
the imposed flux returns $Nu$. The limit $X\to\infty$ follows from the decay of every $n\ge1$
term, and $f(1)=13/35$ gives $Nu_\infty=70/13=5.3846$. Term-by-term differentiation is
legitimate because $\sum\lambda_n^2|A_n|^2<\infty$, which also bounds the truncation error by
the first omitted term.
